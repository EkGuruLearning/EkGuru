Formal and informal Telugu

మీరు or నువ్వు? రండి or రా? Respect levels, decoded.

🔊 Tap any speaker button to hear Telugu spoken. Too fast or slow? Use the speed button at the bottom-right of the page.

Telugu has two yous: మీరు (formal/plural — strangers, elders, officials) and నువ్వు (familiar — friends, children, family). English erased this distinction centuries ago; Telugu keeps it razor-sharp. Every conversation opens with an invisible choice — make it consciously, and make it respectful.

Verbs mirror the split: రండి (please come) versus రా (come!), చెప్పండి (please say) versus చెప్పు (say!), వెళ్లండి (please go) versus వెళ్ళు (go!). The -అండి ending is pure respect — same verb, different universe. Stem-alone orders bark; -అండి invites.

Giving and forgiving show the range: ఇవ్వండి (please give) versus ఇవ్వు (give!), క్షమించండి (please forgive) versus క్షమించు (forgive, familiar). Beggars and kings both hear ఇవ్వండి — politeness in Telugu costs nothing and buys everything.

Strangers get titles: అయ్యా (sir), అమ్మగారు (madam), అన్నయ్య (brother), అక్కయ్య (sister). అయ్యా, సహాయం చేయండి (sir, please help) opens any door; barking orders at strangers closes them. Telugu warmth is earned with two syllables of respect.

A note on గారు. గారు (gaaru) is Telugu's respect rocket — attach it to any name and watch warmth ignite: అమ్మగారు (respected mother/madam), అయ్యగారు (respected sir), రాముగారు. Two syllables that upgrade every introduction instantly.

Questions learners ask

When do I use మీరు?

మీరు for every adult you don't know well — shopkeepers, officials, elders, strangers. నువ్వు waits for close friends, children and family. Wrong-side errors embarrass; learners are forgiven, but respect first is always safest.

How do I make a command polite?

Add -అండి: రా becomes రండి, వెళ్ళు becomes వెళ్లండి, చెప్పు becomes చెప్పండి. One ending, instant politeness — the highest-value suffix in Telugu.

How do I address strangers?

అయ్యా (sir) and అమ్మగారు (madam) — or add గారు (gaaru) to any name: రాముగారు, లక్ష్మిగారు. Better than pointing and shouting in any language.
