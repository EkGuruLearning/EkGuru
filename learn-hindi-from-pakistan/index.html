Why people in Pakistan learn Hindi

This is the unusual case where you may already speak the language. Spoken Hindi and spoken Urdu are, at the everyday level, the same language — the differences are the script, and the direction each draws formal vocabulary from (Sanskrit for Hindi, Persian and Arabic for Urdu). So a Pakistani learner is generally not learning to speak Hindi. They are learning to read Devanagari and to recognise the Sanskritic register used in Indian news, government and literature. That is a much shorter project, and it should be sold to you as one.

Two honest caveats. That figure uses standard time and ignores daylight saving, which moves it by an hour for part of the year in many countries; and some countries span several zones. A note on the Hindi spellings

Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong when it is typed by hand — a missing matra, the wrong nukta. If something here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page and please tell us so it gets fixed — it takes a moment and a real person reads it. Romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard: there is no single official way to write Hindi in Latin letters.

A realistic first month from Pakistan

  1. Week 1 — sound before script. Learn to hear the four-way consonant distinction before you learn to write anything. Most courses do this the other way round and it is why so many learners read Hindi with an accent that never goes away.
  2. Week 2 — the alphabet, properly. Thirty-three consonants and eleven vowels, grouped by where they are made in the mouth. The explorer shows each with a real word.
  3. Week 3 — sentences, not words. Hindi puts the verb last. Read the sentence structure guide once and then build twenty sentences of your own; the second part is the part that works.
  4. Week 4 — speak to a person. Everything above can be done alone. This cannot, and it is the step that decides whether you continue.
