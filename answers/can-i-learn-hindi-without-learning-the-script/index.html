Can I learn Hindi without learning the Devanagari script?

Yes, and for some goals it is the right choice — if you only want to speak with family or handle a trip, spoken Hindi through romanisation is a legitimate path and no tutor should refuse it. But it costs you more than people expect: romanised Hindi has no standard spelling, so you end up learning many words twice, and almost all good material beyond beginner level is in Devanagari.
A note on the Hindi spellings

Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong when it is typed by hand — a missing matra, the wrong nukta. If something here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page and please tell us so it gets fixed. Romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard.

This question is usually asked apologetically, as though skipping the script is cheating. It is not. But the trade-off is real and worth seeing clearly before you choose.

When skipping the script is genuinely reasonable

You want to talk to your in-laws. Spoken comprehension and production. You will never need to read a menu.

You are going to India for three weeks. Two hundred spoken phrases will serve you far better than four weeks on the alphabet.

You already speak Urdu. You have the language; you are choosing which script to add, and Nastaliq may be more useful to you.

You have dyslexia or a visual impairment that makes a new script genuinely costly.

In all of these, say so in your first lesson. A tutor who insists on the alphabet regardless is not listening to you.

What it actually costs

Romanisation has no standard. नहीं is written nahin, nahi, nahee, nheen and nhi, all in common use. You learn a word, meet it spelled differently, and do not recognise it. This is the largest hidden cost and it compounds.

Romanisation cannot show the sounds that matter. त and ट are both written "t". द and ड are both "d". These are different letters that change meaning, and the romanisation erases the distinction — so you cannot even see that there is something to hear. This is the deepest problem.

Material dries up fast. Beginner phrasebooks are romanised. Almost nothing at intermediate level is. You will hit a wall around month six with nothing to read.

You cannot use a dictionary properly. Hindi dictionaries are ordered by Devanagari.

The middle path most people should take

Learn to READ Devanagari without learning to WRITE it.

Reading is about four to eight weeks at twenty minutes a day. Handwriting is a separate skill that takes months and that you may genuinely never need — typing exists.

This gets you dictionaries, signage, subtitles, real materials and the ability to see the retroflex distinction, at a fraction of the cost.

If you still choose to skip it

Be consistent. Pick one romanisation system and stick to it, ideally IAST or your tutor's, and write your own notes the same way every time. Most of the pain comes from inconsistency rather than from romanisation itself.

And revisit the decision at month three. Many people who skip the script find that once they can hold a simple conversation, the alphabet suddenly looks like four weeks well spent rather than a barrier at the start.
