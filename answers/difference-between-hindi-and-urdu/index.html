What is the difference between Hindi and Urdu?

In everyday speech there is almost none — a Hindi speaker and an Urdu speaker discussing food, family or the weather understand each other completely. The differences are the script (Devanagari versus a Perso-Arabic script written right to left) and the formal vocabulary, which Hindi draws from Sanskrit and Urdu from Persian and Arabic. Linguists often treat them as one language, Hindustani, with two literary standards.

This is one of the most misunderstood questions about either language, and the answer matters practically: if you learn one, you have largely learned the other.

The everyday language is the same

Take a normal sentence — asking for tea, telling someone you are tired, arranging to meet tomorrow. In Hindi and Urdu that sentence is word-for-word identical. Same grammar, same word order, same postpositions, same verb endings, same core vocabulary.

Bollywood films are the clearest evidence. They are watched without subtitles in both India and Pakistan because the dialogue is neither pure Hindi nor pure Urdu — it is the shared everyday register both audiences speak.

The script is the real difference

Hindi uses Devanagari, written left to right, with every vowel marked.

Urdu uses a modified Perso-Arabic script written right to left, usually in the flowing Nastaliq style, and short vowels are generally not written at all — you supply them from knowing the word.

This is the difference that actually costs time. Someone fluent in spoken Urdu still needs several weeks to read Hindi, and vice versa.

Formal vocabulary diverges

The higher the register, the further apart they get.

For "country", conversational speech uses mulk in both. Formal Hindi prefers rashtra from Sanskrit; formal Urdu prefers mulk from Arabic. In a news broadcast, a legal document or a poem, the two can become genuinely hard for the other's speaker to follow.

The practical consequences for a learner

If you speak Urdu, you already speak Hindi. You need the script and some Sanskrit-derived formal vocabulary. That is weeks of work, not years.

If you are learning from scratch, choose by purpose. India and Bollywood point to Hindi. Pakistan, Islamic scholarship or Urdu poetry point to Urdu. For travel across South Asia generally, either works.

Do not learn both scripts at once. Pick one, get comfortable, then add the other. Learning two writing systems for one spoken language simultaneously is a well-known way to confuse yourself.

A note on the politics

The split is more political than linguistic and became sharper after 1947. Before that, Hindustani in both scripts was widely understood as one language. Some people feel strongly about this. As a learner you do not have to take a position — but it is worth knowing the question is sensitive.
