What does namaste actually mean?

Roughly "I bow to you" — नमः (a bow) plus ते (to you). In India it is an ordinary polite greeting, not the spiritual formula it has become in yoga studios abroad.
A note on the Hindi spellings

Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong when it is typed by hand — a missing matra, the wrong nukta. If something here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page and please tell us so it gets fixed. Romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard.

The etymology is genuinely as described: a Sanskrit compound meaning something close to "salutation to you". What has drifted is the usage.

How it is actually used in India

As a normal, slightly formal hello. You say it to a shopkeeper, a colleague's parents, a stranger. It is respectful and neutral — exactly what a learner needs, because it is never wrong.

What it is not is intimate. Friends do not greet each other with नमस्ते; they use a name, or hi, or nothing. Using it with a close friend sounds oddly ceremonious, in about the way "good day to you" would in English.

What it does not mean

The phrase "the divine in me bows to the divine in you" is a yoga-studio elaboration, not the everyday meaning. It is not offensive, and it is not what a person in Delhi has in mind when they say it to the person selling them vegetables.

The variants

नमस्कार is a shade more formal and common in broadcasting. आदाब and सलाम are used in Muslim contexts, sat sri akal among Sikhs. Matching what the other person says is both the safe rule and the courteous one.
