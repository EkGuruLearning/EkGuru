German Grammar Basics — Articles, Cases and Word Order

Written by: AI-assisted draft for owner review. Maintained by Prakash. Reviewed by: not recorded. Updated: 2026-10-05. Editorial policy and corrections.

Scope and pronunciation: Examples use Standard German. German is already written in the Latin alphabet; there is no transliteration here. Any English-friendly sound cue is a learner aid, not IPA. Regional speech may differ from the written models.

German nouns carry gender and capital letters

Every German noun has grammatical gender: masculine, feminine, or neuter. Learn the article with the noun—der Tisch (“the table”), die Lampe (“the lamp”), das Buch (“the book”). A noun’s gender is a property of that word, not a reliable description of the object. Some endings are useful clues, but exceptions are common enough that a dictionary and a memory card should keep the article visible.

Nouns are capitalized in Standard German writing, including in the middle of a sentence: Das Buch liegt hier. (“The book is here.”) Capitalization makes long German sentences easier to scan because it helps the reader spot nouns. German also capitalizes some words that are nominalized from verbs or adjectives. Start with ordinary nouns and notice the visual pattern as you read; do not assume every capital letter is a proper name.

Cases show what a noun is doing

German uses four grammatical cases in the standard system. Nominative usually marks the subject; accusative commonly marks a direct object; dative appears with certain verbs and prepositions and often marks an indirect object; genitive often expresses possession in formal writing. Case is shown through articles and some endings. The form changes most clearly in masculine singular articles: der in the nominative becomes den in the accusative and dem in the dative.

Do not try to learn every declension table before you can make a simple sentence. Notice one contrast: Der Mann sieht den Hund. means “The man sees the dog,” while Der Hund sieht den Mann. means “The dog sees the man.” The article helps identify who is doing the seeing. Learn the pattern with a few common nouns and expand it as your reading requires.

Articles, adjective endings, and noun phrases

German articles and adjectives change together with gender, case, and number. Compare ein guter Kaffee (“a good coffee,” masculine nominative), einen guten Kaffee (masculine accusative), and mit einem guten Kaffee (“with a good coffee,” dative after mit). You do not need to guess the ending from the English translation; identify the noun’s gender, the phrase’s role, and the article already present.

Build a phrase in layers. First learn der Kaffee. Then add a descriptive adjective, then put the phrase into a short sentence. A table can help explain the pattern, but examples make it usable. If you are unsure, check the phrase in a trusted Standard German dictionary or grammar resource and note the whole model sentence, not just the final letter.

Main clauses put the conjugated verb in second position

In a basic German main clause, the conjugated verb normally occupies the second position. “Second position” means the second sentence element, not necessarily the second word. Ich lerne heute Deutsch. (“I am learning German today.”) begins with the subject. Heute lerne ich Deutsch. (“Today I am learning German.”) begins with a time word, and the subject moves after the verb. Both are grammatical; the choice can change what the speaker emphasizes.

Yes-or-no questions often put the conjugated verb first: Lernst du Deutsch? (“Are you learning German?”) A question word usually takes the first position and the verb follows: Wo wohnst du? (“Where do you live?”) Practise these as separate sentence frames. If you move every German verb to the end because of a rule you heard, you will make ordinary main clauses sound incorrect.

Subordinate clauses and two-verb phrases

Subordinate clauses introduced by words such as weil (“because”) commonly send the finite verb to the end: Ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Berlin wohne. (“I am learning German because I live in Berlin.”) The main clause keeps its usual verb position; the subordinate clause has its own pattern. Other subordinating conjunctions, including dass (“that”), use a similar structure in Standard German.

With a modal verb, the conjugated modal is normally in the second position and the other verb appears as an infinitive at the end: Ich kann heute arbeiten. (“I can work today.”) Separable verbs can also create a bracket around part of the sentence. These patterns are easier to understand by marking the finite verb and the infinitive in real examples. Start with one clause at a time, then combine them after the basic positions feel familiar.

Prepositions and the difference between location and direction

Some prepositions require a particular case. For example, mit takes the dative and für takes the accusative: mit dem Bus (“by bus”) and für den Freund (“for the friend”). Other prepositions, such as in, can use either accusative or dative depending on meaning. A location question often pairs with dative; a direction or change of location often pairs with accusative.

Compare Ich bin in der Schule. (“I am at school.”) with Ich gehe in die Schule. (“I am going to school.”) The first describes a location; the second describes movement toward a place. English often uses the same preposition for both, which can hide the German case distinction. Learn the pair as a contrast and consult a course when a specific verb or idiom has its own pattern.

Negation: nicht and kein

Nicht can negate a verb phrase, adjective, or sentence: Ich bin nicht müde. means “I am not tired.” Kein negates a noun phrase that would otherwise have an indefinite article or no article: Ich habe kein Auto. means “I do not have a car.” It changes ending like ein: keine Frage (“no question”), for example. The placement of nicht depends on what is being negated, so learn it in a complete sentence.

Common grammar mistakes to catch early

Beginners often learn nouns without articles, put a main-clause verb at the end, or use the nominative article after every preposition. These are understandable shortcuts, but they obscure who does what to whom. Another mistake is treating adjective endings as random. They reflect a pattern of information carried by the article and the noun phrase. Instead of memorizing a sentence as a list of separate words, mark the noun, its role, the article, and the finite verb.

Do not mix informal and formal address without noticing: du and Sie use different verb forms. Also avoid assuming that a textbook’s Standard German sentence is the only way a person might phrase the idea in everyday speech. Regional vocabulary and spoken constructions vary. Label the model you are learning, then ask a fluent speaker whether it fits your situation.

Practice: build a grammar ladder

Start with der Kaffee. Make it a subject: Der Kaffee ist heiß. (“The coffee is hot.”) Make it a direct object: Ich trinke den Kaffee. (“I drink the coffee.”) Add a dative phrase: Ich sitze mit dem Kaffee am Tisch. (“I am sitting at the table with the coffee.”) Finally, move a time phrase to the start: Heute trinke ich den Kaffee. (“Today I am drinking the coffee.”)

Underline each verb, circle the noun phrase, and identify the article form. Say every sentence aloud and listen to a Standard German model. Then replace one noun with a feminine or neuter example and check the endings. This small ladder practises case and word order without asking you to solve every part of German grammar at once.
