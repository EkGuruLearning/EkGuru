German Numbers 1 to 100 — Counting, Prices and Time

Written by: AI-assisted draft for owner review. Maintained by Prakash. Reviewed by: not recorded. Updated: 2026-10-05. Editorial policy and corrections.

Pronunciation and scope: German uses the Latin alphabet, so these numbers need no transliteration. Any English-friendly sound hint is a learner aid, not IPA. The spellings below are Standard German; spoken shortcuts, number rhythm, and time expressions can vary regionally.

Count from eins to zwölf

Learn the first twelve as individual words. Notice the umlaut in fünf and zwölf, and keep the spelling visible while you listen. A number list is only a starting point: practise saying an address, ticket number, or quantity in a complete sentence. German numbers are written with the same left-to-right alphabet as English, even when they appear inside a German sentence.

Numbers thirteen to nineteen mostly combine a unit with zehn. Watch for two shortened forms: 16 is sechzehn, not a fully repeated sechs form, and 17 is siebzehn. These spellings are easier to learn as whole words and check in audio than to derive from an English rule. The sequence continues with dreizehn, vierzehn, fünfzehn, achtzehn, and neunzehn.

The number zero is null. You will see it in temperatures, scores, and spoken digit strings. A standalone count uses eins, but compound numbers use ein; this contrast is clearer if you write a short pair in your notebook. Keep spelling and pronunciation together: the alphabet is familiar, but that does not make every letter sound English.

Build the round tens

Learn 20, 30, and the other tens before combining them. Several spellings are worth special attention: 30 is dreißig; 60 is sechzig; and 70 is siebzig. In Germany and Austria, ß is part of the standard spelling in dreißig; Swiss Standard German uses ss. The words for 40, 50, 80, and 90 are formed regularly enough to remember with a short rhythm drill.

Large numbers and thousands

After hundert (“hundred”) comes tausend (“thousand”). A year such as 2024 can be said as zweitausendvierundzwanzig. Written compounds may look long, so divide them into the thousands, hundreds, and units while you practise, then write the standard compound as one word. In everyday conversation, a speaker may choose a shorter or context-dependent way to give a year; listen to current local usage instead of insisting that one reading is universal.

For addresses, telephone numbers, booking codes, and reference numbers, people often say digits individually or in small groups rather than pronounce them as a large cardinal number. Practices vary by context and region. When accuracy matters, repeat the digits aloud or show the written number to the other person. Distinguish a phone number from a quantity: use the format the listener expects and confirm it before ending the exchange.

The units come before the tens

From 21 through 99, German normally names the unit first, then und (“and”), and then the tens. Twenty-one is einundzwanzig, literally “one-and-twenty.” Thirty-four is vierunddreißig, and ninety-nine is neunundneunzig. The number is written as one compound word. This backward order is one of the biggest surprises for English learners, so say the unit first as you build the word.

When “one” is part of a compound number, the form is ein, as in einundzwanzig, not einsundzwanzig. For a standalone count, use eins. Before a noun, the form depends on the noun phrase: ein Euro (“one euro”) but eine Fahrkarte (“one ticket,” feminine noun). Learn the complete phrase, because the article and number can reflect gender and case.

Use numbers for age, time, and dates

For age, German uses Ich bin zwanzig Jahre alt. (“I am twenty years old.”) The phrase is close to English but includes alt (“old”). To ask someone’s age informally, a model is Wie alt bist du?; in a formal exchange, use Wie alt sind Sie? Personal questions are not appropriate in every situation, so follow the context rather than practising the sentence on every stranger.

For clock time, Es ist drei Uhr. means “It is three o’clock.” The expression halb drei commonly refers to 2:30 in Standard German usage: it is halfway to three. Some regions use different quarter-hour expressions, so verify a local time convention before relying on it for an appointment. German written dates often use ordinal forms; am ersten Mai means “on the first of May.” Learn month names separately and check the date order on travel documents.

Prices, receipts, and regional formatting

When asking for a price, Wie viel kostet das? means “How much does that cost?” In many German-language settings, a decimal comma appears in writing, for example 12,50 Euro. Currency and thousands separators vary by country and document, so read the full receipt and check the currency symbol. Switzerland uses Swiss francs, while Germany and Austria use euros; a German-language price does not tell you which currency is meant.

When you hear a number, repeat it back or show the written amount if the transaction matters. A long number can be difficult to catch in a station or shop, especially with unfamiliar regional pronunciation. Ask Können Sie die Zahl bitte wiederholen? (“Could you please repeat the number?” in a formal address). Written digits are often clearer than guessing from a sound, and a quick check prevents errors with bookings and payments.

Common number mistakes to avoid

The first common mistake is reversing the order of units and tens because English says “twenty-one.” Remember to build einundzwanzig as one-and-twenty. Another is writing or saying eins in a compound, where Standard German uses ein. A third is overlooking the special spellings sechzehn, siebzehn, sechzig, and siebzig.

Do not confuse the number’s spelling with a local way of telling time, nor assume every German-speaking region writes a price in exactly the same format. Keep a source label on your notes and check a current destination guide for currency and date conventions. The goal is accurate communication, not memorizing a single format and applying it everywhere.

Practice: count, combine, and check

First count from one to twelve without looking at the table. Then say the teens, pausing briefly on 16 and 17 to check their shortened forms. Next combine three units with tens: 21, 34, and 58. Write each German number as one word and underline the unit part, und, and the tens part. Read the compound after a Standard German audio model, then cover the spelling and try again.

Finish by role-playing a café purchase: ask Wie viel kostet das?, hear a price such as zwölf Euro fünfzig, and repeat it back. Use a current rate source if you need to convert the currency; a language lesson does not provide live exchange information.
