How to Say Hello in Hindi — And When Namaste Is the Wrong Word

A free guide from EkGuru · updated 2026-09-11

A note on the Hindi spellings

These guides are written and typed by hand, and Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong — a missing matra, the wrong nukta, a halant in the wrong place. If a word here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page, and please tell us so it gets fixed. The romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard — Hindi has no single agreed romanisation, so you will see shukriya and shukriyaa both used.

Every guidebook tells you to say नमस्ते (namaste). That is not wrong, but it is incomplete, and using it in the wrong place marks you instantly as someone reading from a phrasebook.

Namaste — what it actually means

नमस्ते comes from namah (I bow) and te (to you). It is respectful, it works at any hour of the day, and it is never rude. Press your palms together at chest height and dip your head slightly.

नमस्कार (namaskaar) is the same idea, slightly more formal. Use it with older people, in offices, or when you want to show extra respect.

When namaste is the wrong word

SituationSay this instead
Greeting a Muslim friend or familyसलाम वालेकुम (salaam waalekum)
Greeting a Sikhसत श्री अकाल (sat shri akaal)
Close friends, your own ageक्या हाल है? (kya haal hai?) — what's up
Answering the phoneहैलो (hello) — yes, really
Walking into a shopoften nothing at all; just state what you want

That last row surprises people. Indian shops are not places for elaborate greeting rituals. A nod and "bhaiya, ye kitne ka hai?" is perfectly normal.

The follow-up question, which matters more

Greeting someone and then falling silent is awkward in any language. Learn one of these and you have an actual conversation:

  • आप कैसे हैं? (aap kaise hain?) — how are you? Formal, to a man
  • आप कैसी हैं? (aap kaisi hain?) — Formal, to a woman
  • तुम कैसे हो? (tum kaise ho?) — Informal, to a friend
  • क्या हाल है? (kya haal hai?) — Casual, "how's it going"

Notice that the greeting changes depending on whether you are speaking to a man or a woman. Hindi verbs and adjectives agree with gender, and this catches out almost every English speaker. It is not optional politeness — it is grammar, and getting it wrong sounds as odd as "he are going" does in English.

Answering

मैं ठीक हूँ, धन्यवाद (main theek hoon, dhanyavaad) — I am fine, thank you.

In everyday speech most people say शुक्रिया (shukriya) rather than dhanyavaad, which can sound stiff or overly formal between friends. And often nobody says thank you at all for small things — among family it can even feel cold and distancing. This is the kind of thing no app tells you.

Time-of-day greetings

Hindi has सुप्रभात (suprabhaat, good morning) and शुभ रात्रि (shubh raatri, good night), but be aware: these are formal, literary words. You will hear them on television news, rarely between friends. In practice people say namaste at any hour, or simply "good morning" in English, which is completely normal in Indian cities.

Saying goodbye

  • फिर मिलेंगे (phir milenge) — we'll meet again. Warm, natural.
  • अलविदा (alvida) — goodbye, but final. Suitable for a long parting, odd for leaving the office.
  • नमस्ते — works for goodbye too.

Getting greetings right is the fastest way to be treated as someone making a genuine effort rather than a tourist. It takes one lesson.
