Arabic Numbers 1 to 100 — Digits, Words and Counting

Written by: AI-assisted draft for owner review. Maintained by Prakash. Reviewed by: not recorded. Updated: 2026-10-05. Editorial policy and corrections.

Transliteration and scope: The Latin spellings below are practical learner aids, not IPA or one universal standard. The word forms are Modern Standard Arabic (MSA). Number endings can change with grammar, and counted nouns affect the form of some numbers; learn each form with its example rather than memorising a bare list alone.

Arabic words and Arabic-Indic digits are different tools

Arabic numerals appear in more than one written shape. The familiar Western digits 0–9 are used in many Arabic-speaking contexts, while Arabic-Indic digits also appear on clocks, documents, signs, and educational materials in parts of the region. The shapes shown below are Arabic-Indic: ٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩. Their order here represents zero through nine. Actual usage varies by country, institution, device, and situation, so do not assume a sign will use only one set.

Arabic text runs right to left, but numbers in mixed text have their own display behaviour. A browser may show a phone number or price left to right within an Arabic sentence. Practise recognising both digit sets and confirm the order of a long number from context. For travel, copy a booking code exactly and compare it with the original; do not reverse digits because the surrounding sentence is Arabic.

Count from one to ten

Arabic number forms interact with grammatical gender. One and two generally agree with the counted noun, and in many everyday phrases the noun can appear before them. Numbers three through ten use a pattern often called gender polarity: the number form contrasts with the gender of the noun it counts. The system feels backward to beginners because the ending that looks feminine commonly accompanies a masculine noun in that range. Learn a number together with a noun, not as an isolated translation.

Notice how the examples change with the noun. كتاب واحد (kitāb wāḥid, “one book”) uses a masculine noun, while سيارة واحدة (sayyāra wāḥida, “one car”) uses a feminine noun. For three, ثلاثة كتب (thalāthat kutub, “three books”) contrasts with ثلاث سيارات (thalāth sayyārāt, “three cars”). The noun forms and their formal case endings can be more detailed than this unvowelled learner spelling; use a grammar source when you need a fully inflected sentence.

From eleven to twenty

For eleven through nineteen, Arabic combines a unit with a form of “ten.” The counted noun is commonly singular in these phrases, and the number’s parts show gender patterns. For a masculine counted noun, useful MSA forms include أحد عشر (aḥada ʿashara, eleven), اثنا عشر (ithnā ʿashara, twelve), and ثلاثة عشر (thalāthata ʿashara, thirteen). The exact ending of twelve changes with grammatical case, so a table gives a starting point rather than every possible sentence form.

Feminine counted nouns have corresponding forms such as إحدى عشرة (iḥdā ʿashra, eleven) and ثلاث عشرة (thalātha ʿashra, thirteen). The endings can look similar when short vowels are not written, which is one reason it helps to practise with fully vowelled examples and audio. Do not try to infer a speaker’s gender from a number phrase or apply the masculine column to every noun.

Count by tens to one hundred

The round tens are more regular than the teen forms. In the nominative form used in a simple vocabulary list, MSA uses twenty, thirty, forty, and so on. These forms combine with the unit using the conjunction “and.” In written compound numbers from twenty-one to ninety-nine, the unit is stated first, followed by “and” and the tens: واحد وعشرون (wāḥid wa ʿishrūn, twenty-one, with masculine agreement) and خمسة وثلاثون (khamsa wa thalāthūn, thirty-five in a masculine counting pattern). Case can change the ending of the tens.

Arabic has spelling variants for “one hundred,” including مئة and مائة. You may see either in modern writing. Learn the form used by your course, then recognise the other rather than assuming it is a different number. When the number modifies a noun, the noun’s form and the sentence’s case add another layer; consult a full example before using a number in formal writing.

Prices, age, time, and dates

For a price, كم السعر؟ (kam as-siʿr?, “How much is the price?”) is a useful formal question. The currency depends on the country: Arabic-speaking regions use different units and subunits, so learn the local name and check displayed digits. A number phrase on a receipt may use Western or Arabic-Indic digits. Compare the currency symbol or word as well as the amount; never infer an exchange rate from a language lesson.

Age is often expressed with a phrase meaning “my age,” such as عمري عشرون سنة (ʿumrī ʿishrūn sana, “I am twenty years old”). To ask someone’s age, a common MSA model is كم عمرك؟ (kam ʿumruka? addressed to a man; the spoken ending differs for a woman). Time can be asked with كم الساعة؟ (kam as-sāʿa?, “What time is it?”). For dates, learn the month names and whether your document writes the date day-first or another way; context and local convention matter.

Common number mistakes to avoid

Do not memorise a number without its counted noun. One and two generally agree with the noun, while three through ten follow the familiar polarity pattern; copying the masculine form onto a feminine noun can change the phrase. Also avoid assuming that every 11–99 construction takes the same noun form as numbers three through ten. The counted noun is commonly singular after 11–99, and formal case endings depend on the sentence.

Do not reverse Arabic-Indic digits just because the sentence is right-to-left, and do not assume one country uses one digit set everywhere. A receipt may use Western digits while a clock or sign uses Arabic-Indic shapes. Check the full amount, currency, and source. Transliteration is not IPA, and its spelling can conceal distinctions, so listen to a trusted MSA model for number words you intend to say aloud.

Practice counting with gender and rhythm

First, count from one to ten while pointing to each number word. Then pick two nouns with different grammatical genders and practise one, two, and three of each. Use كتاب واحد and سيارة واحدة, then compare ثلاثة كتب and ثلاث سيارات. Say each phrase after a trusted MSA recording. This makes gender agreement part of the number rather than a separate rule you forget in conversation.

Next, build a number from a unit and a ten: say twenty-one, thirty-five, and ninety-nine in words, then check the spelling. Read Arabic-Indic digits and Western digits in both directions. Keep a small daily practice: count objects at home, say a price, or tell yourself the time. Transliteration can help you begin, but it is not IPA, and the final vowels in formal MSA may be left out in ordinary speech or learner spelling.
