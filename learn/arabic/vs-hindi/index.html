Arabic vs Hindi — Scripts, Grammar and What Transfers

Written by: AI-assisted draft for owner review. Maintained by Prakash. Reviewed by: not recorded. Updated: 2026-10-05. Editorial policy and corrections.

Scope and transliteration: This comparison focuses on Hindi as commonly taught in Devanagari and on Modern Standard Arabic (MSA). Latin-letter Arabic forms are learner transliteration, not IPA or a single standard. Hindi and Arabic are distinct languages with their own histories; shared words and learning strategies do not make them the same language.

Different language families and histories

Arabic belongs to the Semitic branch of the Afro-Asiatic language family. Hindi is an Indo-Aryan language within the Indo-European family. Their core grammatical histories are therefore different, even though people and words have travelled between Arabic-speaking and South Asian communities for centuries. A familiar word is a useful memory hook, not proof that an entire sentence follows the same grammar.

Hindi has been used in multilingual communities and registers, and the label “Hindi” covers varied speech across regions. MSA is a formal written and spoken register used across countries, alongside many regional Arabic dialects. A learner should compare a named variety with a named variety: standard Hindi with MSA, or a regional Hindi speaker with a particular Arabic dialect. “Hindi versus Arabic” cannot represent every speaker or context in either language.

Two writing systems: Devanagari and Arabic script

Standard Hindi is commonly written in Devanagari from left to right. Arabic is written from right to left in a connected script. Hindi’s letters have a headline that often runs across a word, while Arabic letters change their shape depending on whether they stand alone or join a neighbour. Arabic has no capital letters. These visual differences are easiest to learn through daily reading, not by comparing alphabet charts once.

Devanagari is an abugida: consonant letters carry an inherent vowel that can be changed or cancelled with marks. Arabic writing usually represents consonants and long vowels, while short-vowel marks are often left out of ordinary text. That means a beginner may need to know more vocabulary and grammar to read an unvowelled Arabic word than to sound out a fully marked classroom example. Both scripts reward attention to small details; in Devanagari, vowel signs and the headline matter, while Arabic learners must watch dots, joining, and the position of a word.

Try comparing a greeting: Hindi नमस्ते (namaste) and Arabic مرحبا (marḥaban). The scripts look and move differently, and the phrase belongs to a different cultural and linguistic context. Learning both scripts is possible; do not transliterate one into the other and expect the original sound to survive.

Word order: Hindi often places the verb last

Hindi commonly uses subject–object–verb order: मैं किताब पढ़ता हूँ (main kitāb paṛhtā hū̃, “I read a book,” in a masculine speaker form). MSA often uses verb–subject–object in a neutral formal sentence, while subject–verb–object is also common and useful. For example, يقرأ الطالب الكتاب (yaqraʾu aṭ-ṭālib al-kitāb) places the verb before the subject and object. You cannot simply move every Arabic verb to the final position because that is the familiar Hindi pattern.

Hindi uses postpositions after a noun phrase, such as घर में (ghar mẽ, “in the house”). Arabic commonly uses prepositions before the noun phrase, as in في البيت (fī al-bayt, “in the house”). Word order is not a cage: emphasis, register, and the sentence type matter in both languages. Read complete examples and notice where each language places the relationship word.

Both have grammatical gender, but do not copy the rules

Hindi and Arabic both use grammatical gender, so a Hindi learner may already expect adjectives or verbs to change form. That familiarity can help you pay attention to agreement. The details are different. Hindi has masculine and feminine nouns and agreement patterns; Arabic also distinguishes masculine and feminine and marks gender on many pronouns, adjectives, and verb forms. Arabic has a dual number for exactly two in formal grammar, while Hindi normally expresses “two” through its number word and noun phrase rather than a general dual paradigm.

For a simple MSA contrast, كتاب جديد (kitāb jadīd, “a new book”) and مدينة جديدة (madīna jadīda, “a new city”) show an adjective following its noun and changing with grammatical gender. Hindi adjective placement and agreement work differently. Do not map a Hindi feminine ending directly onto Arabic or infer a person’s identity from a grammatical category. Learn each noun with an example and treat gender as a property of the grammar.

Arabic roots and familiar South Asian vocabulary

Many Arabic words are built from consonantal roots combined with patterns. That can make related words recognisable once a learner knows the root, though the system is not a simple code in which every meaning can be guessed. Hindi vocabulary includes Arabic-origin words, often through Persian and long histories of contact. For instance, Hindi किताब (kitāb, “book”) is related historically to Arabic كتاب (kitāb). Other familiar words may have travelled through Persian, Urdu, or more than one route, and meanings can shift.

Hindi and Urdu share much everyday Hindustani grammar, while standardisation, scripts, and formal vocabulary differ in ways shaped by history and society. Urdu is not simply Arabic written in a different script, and Arabic is not a South Asian language. If a word looks familiar, check its pronunciation, meaning, and current register in both languages. A cognate can speed up vocabulary learning but cannot tell you how the word behaves in a sentence.

Sounds each learner may find unfamiliar

A Hindi speaker may bring useful experience with distinctions that many English-only learners have not practised, including dental and retroflex consonants. Arabic has its own contrasts, including emphatic consonants and sounds such as ع (ʿayn) and ح (ḥā), which do not have exact Hindi or English equivalents. Similar-looking transliteration symbols do not guarantee identical sounds across language systems. Listen to MSA speakers and ask a teacher to model a sound rather than assuming a familiar South Asian articulation is a perfect match.

Arabic dialects can pronounce some letters differently from careful MSA and from one another. If your goal is an Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf, or other variety, use a source for that variety. Hindi itself has regional pronunciation differences too, so the comparison is between actual speakers, not an idealised single accent. Mark the variety on your recording and keep an open ear.

What transfers—and what does not

Useful transferable habits include learning a script systematically, listening for distinctions, recording yourself, studying grammatical agreement, and reviewing vocabulary with spaced recall. Your Hindi study may also make you less afraid of changing word forms and give you access to some shared historical vocabulary. What does not transfer automatically is the script, the sound value of a letter, a verb pattern, or the ordinary word order. Treat each apparent similarity as a hypothesis to check.

A practical notebook can use four columns: Hindi example, Arabic example, the feature being compared, and one difference you noticed. For example, contrast Hindi postposition order with the Arabic preposition in घर में and في البيت. Add a note that Hindi usually reads left-to-right while Arabic reads right-to-left. This turns comparison into an active study tool rather than a claim that one language is easy because another is known.

Common comparison mistakes to avoid

A Hindi learner may see a shared word or gender category and assume the grammar transfers. It does not: the writing systems, word order, agreement details, and verb patterns have their own rules. Another mistake is calling Urdu “Arabic in a different script” or treating Hindi and Arabic as religious identities. Those claims flatten distinct linguistic histories and the choices speakers make about language, register, and script.

Do not infer a word’s origin from a familiar sound alone. Hindi vocabulary has several historical routes of contact, and some words travelled through Persian or Urdu before reaching a Hindi register. Check a reputable dictionary before calling a pair a direct cognate. Comparison is most useful when you name exactly what matches, what differs, and what you still need to verify.

Practice: make a careful side-by-side card

Choose one meaning, such as “I am at home.” Write a natural Hindi sentence and a simple MSA phrase with help from a trusted course, then label every word’s role. Remember that a present-tense Arabic nominal sentence may omit a separate “am/is,” whereas Hindi uses its own copula patterns. Read the Arabic from right to left and say it aloud after a recording. Do not force the words into matching positions just to make the lines look similar.

Then choose one shared-looking word, such as kitāb, and compare spelling, pronunciation, plural or agreement, and register. Write down what is genuinely shared and what is not.
