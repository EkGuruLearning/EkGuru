Arabic Grammar Basics — Sentence Structure Explained

Written by: AI-assisted draft for owner review. Maintained by Prakash. Reviewed by: not recorded. Updated: 2026-10-05. Editorial policy and corrections.

Scope and transliteration: This guide introduces Modern Standard Arabic (MSA). The Latin spellings are learner transliteration, not IPA or one universal system. Spoken dialects may simplify or replace some of these patterns, so do not mix a formal example with a dialect ending unless you know why.

Two useful sentence shapes

Many first Arabic sentences are nominal: they name or describe something without a present-tense equivalent of English “is.” For example, البيت كبير (al-bayt kabīr) means “the house is big.” المدينة جميلة (al-madīna jamīla) means “the city is beautiful.” English learners often insert an extra word because English requires it. In this simple MSA pattern, the noun and its description can stand together without a present copula.

With verbs, MSA commonly uses both verb–subject–object and subject–verb–object orders. يقرأ الطالب الكتاب (yaqraʾu aṭ-ṭālib al-kitāb) means “the student reads the book,” with the verb first. الطالب يقرأ الكتاب (aṭ-ṭālib yaqraʾu al-kitāb) also means “the student reads the book,” putting the subject first. The choice affects focus and, in formal agreement, can affect how the verb matches a plural subject. Learn both from complete sentences rather than treating one order as an error.

Nouns, grammatical gender, and the definite article

Arabic nouns have grammatical gender, commonly masculine or feminine. A word ending in ة often signals a feminine noun, as in مدينة (madīna, “city”), but this is a helpful clue, not a rule without exceptions. Gender is part of the grammar of a word; it does not tell you anything about a person’s identity. When a noun is the subject, related adjectives and some verb forms agree with its grammatical gender.

The definite article is written الـ (al-), roughly “the.” Arabic does not use an indefinite article like English “a” before every singular noun. Compare كتاب (kitāb, “a book” in a suitable context) with الكتاب (al-kitāb, “the book”). Some grammar endings appear in fully formal, vowelled writing and speech but are not normally written in everyday unvowelled text. At beginner level, learn the article with each example and notice the sun-letter pronunciation rule in the alphabet guide.

Adjective agreement comes after the noun

In a simple adjective phrase, the adjective usually follows the noun: بيت كبير (bayt kabīr, “a big house”) and مدينة جميلة (madīna jamīla, “a beautiful city”). When the noun is feminine, the adjective often takes a feminine ending too. When the noun is definite, the adjective normally matches that definiteness: البيت الكبير (al-bayt al-kabīr, “the big house”). This is why a literal English word order can sound wrong even when you know all the vocabulary.

Do not try to memorise every agreement detail from a chart before you can read the phrase. Take one noun, add one adjective, and change one feature at a time. Make pairs such as “a new book / a new city” and “the new book / the new city.” Check each form in a trusted MSA source. This makes agreement visible and gives you a small set of correct patterns to reuse.

Pronouns, verb endings, and the missing subject

Arabic pronouns include distinctions of gender and number. A familiar starter set is أنا (anā, “I”), أنت (anta or anti, “you,” depending on the person addressed), هو (huwa, “he”), هي (hiya, “she”), and نحن (naḥnu, “we”). MSA also has dual forms for two people. A verb often carries enough information about its subject that a separate pronoun is not required; speakers add a pronoun for emphasis, contrast, or clarity.

Present-tense verb forms use prefixes and endings. For a first-person example, أكتب (aktubu) means “I write” in a fully articulated MSA reading. In a short sentence, أكتب رسالة (aktubu risāla) means “I write a message.” Learn the whole form with its subject and an example. Do not assume an English infinitive can simply be placed after every subject; Arabic verbs are built around patterns and change with person, number, and gender.

Build possession with a phrase, not always an “of” word

Arabic often places two nouns next to each other to express possession or a close relationship. This is called an iḍāfa construction. كتاب الطالب (kitāb aṭ-ṭālib) means “the student’s book” or literally “book the student.” The first noun does not take the definite article in this construction; the final noun can make the whole phrase definite. Longer chains are possible, but beginners should start with two nouns and check the pronunciation of the article on the second noun.

Possessive endings also attach to nouns: كتابي (kitābī, “my book”) adds an ending to “book.” Another useful MSA way to express having something is عندي كتاب (ʿindī kitāb, “I have a book,” literally “at me, a book”). Dialects may prefer other constructions. Keep the example’s register clear and avoid assuming that one textbook phrase is the only way every speaker expresses possession.

Questions and negation

Question words are easy to spot once you learn them: من (man, “who”), ما (mā, “what”), أين (ayna, “where”), متى (matā, “when”), and كيف (kayfa, “how”). The particle هل (hal) can introduce a yes-or-no question in formal MSA: هل تفهم؟ (hal tafham?, “Do you understand?” addressed to a man in this form). Intonation and punctuation help, but the written question mark is not the only clue.

Negation depends on the sentence and tense. A useful present-tense pattern is لا أفهم (lā afham, “I do not understand”). A simple nominal sentence can use ليس (laysa, “is not”), which changes according to its grammatical subject. Other past and subjunctive forms have their own particles and effects on the verb. Learn a negative phrase as a model and then study its pattern; replacing every English “not” with the same Arabic word will lead to mistakes.

Common grammar mistakes to catch early

Beginners often insert a present-tense “is” into every nominal sentence, place an adjective before its noun by copying English, or forget to make the adjective agree with grammatical gender. Each is understandable: English and Arabic organise these meanings differently. Another common mistake is to memorise a verb with a pronoun and then assume the pronoun is always required. In Arabic, the verb form often carries subject information, while an explicit pronoun may add emphasis.

Do not label every difference in word order as wrong. MSA allows useful verb-first and subject-first patterns, and regional spoken dialects may have their own preferences. Keep examples tied to a register, practise one contrast at a time, and ask a teacher to explain the sentence rather than merely marking it right or wrong. This builds a model you can reuse.

Practice a grammar ladder

Take one noun and make four steps: identify it, describe it, locate it, and use it in a question. For example: كتاب (kitāb, “book”); كتاب جديد (kitāb jadīd, “a new book”); الكتاب في البيت (al-kitāb fī al-bayt, “the book is in the house”); and أين الكتاب؟ (ayna al-kitāb?, “Where is the book?”). Read each phrase slowly, then cover the English and reconstruct it from meaning.

For a second round, switch to a feminine noun and adjust the adjective. For a third, replace the noun phrase with a verb sentence and decide whether you want the verb or subject first. Keep a note of what changed and why. The aim is not to use every rule in one sentence; it is to notice one reliable relationship at a time. MSA is the course focus, while local spoken dialects may use different word order, endings, and everyday phrases.
