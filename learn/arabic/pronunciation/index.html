The Arabic Alphabet — 28 Letters, Shapes and Sounds

Written by: AI-assisted draft for owner review. Maintained by Prakash. Reviewed by: not recorded. Updated: 2026-10-05. Editorial policy and corrections.

Transliteration note: The Latin forms in this guide are practical learner transliteration, not IPA or a single international spelling standard. Long-vowel marks and the symbols ʿ and ḥ help distinguish Arabic sounds. The model here is careful Modern Standard Arabic; dialects may pronounce some letters differently.

Start with letters as sounds and shapes

The Arabic alphabet has 28 basic letters and is written from right to left. A letter can look different at the start, middle or end of a connected word, but it is still the same letter and usually the same core sound. Make a small chart with four columns: isolated shape, sound, joined form, and a word you know. Learn letters in visually related groups rather than cramming the traditional alphabet song alone. For example, ب ت ث share a basic bowl-like shape and are distinguished by the number and position of dots.

Dots matter. Read them before guessing the letter, and keep them visible when you practise handwriting. Six letters do not connect to the letter that follows them in the written sequence: ا د ذ ر ز و. They may connect from their right side to an earlier letter, but create a break on the left. This explains why a word can contain a visual gap without having a space. Learn that joining rule early; it turns a seemingly complicated script into a small number of predictable shapes.

Listen for the sounds English does not organise

Many Arabic sounds have a rough English neighbour, but an approximation is only a starting point. The letter ع (ʿayn) is produced deep in the throat and has no exact English equivalent. ح (ḥā) is a breathy, constricted h farther back than an ordinary English h. By contrast, ه (hā) is closer to the familiar h. Hear these sounds in multiple words and compare them with a careful MSA recording rather than trying to force a perfect imitation after one listen.

The letter خ (khā) is a rough sound made farther back than English h; some English speakers recognise a similar sound in Scottish “loch.” The letter ق (qāf) is produced farther back than the English k in careful MSA speech. In several spoken dialects, the everyday reflex of this letter differs, so a dialect learner should follow that dialect’s model instead of correcting a speaker toward formal pronunciation.

Notice emphatic consonants and their effect

Arabic distinguishes several consonants that English learners may initially hear as a familiar s, d, t, or z-like sound with a darker resonance. The emphatic group is commonly represented by ص ض ط ظ. In learner transliteration, you may see ṣ, ḍ, ṭ, and ẓ. Do not read those marks as decorative accents: they distinguish letters and can distinguish words. Listen to minimal or near-minimal examples from a trusted audio source and repeat them in a relaxed voice; squeezing the throat or exaggerating the “darkness” can create tension rather than clarity.

Pairs help you train the ear. Compare س (sīn) with ص (ṣād), and ت (tā) with ط (ṭā). Listen first, then look at the spelling, then listen again. A phone recording of your own voice can reveal whether you are hearing the contrast, but it cannot tell you on its own whether your pronunciation is native-like. Use a patient teacher or fluent speaker for feedback when precision matters.

Short vowels, long vowels, and marks

Arabic writing normally shows long vowels with letters such as ا و ي, while short vowels are often omitted from ordinary adult text. Learning books may add small marks above or below a consonant: fatḥa, kasra, and ḍamma, commonly introduced as short a, i, and u. A tiny mark can change a word’s reading, so beginners should practise with clearly vowelled material first. In an unvowelled text, use the sentence, a dictionary, and grammar together rather than believing that each group of consonants has only one possible pronunciation.

Long and short vowels are not interchangeable. In a transliteration, a line over a vowel often signals length, as in ā or ī; the same convention is not used by every book. Hear the length in a recording and connect it to spelling. Read a short vowelled line slowly, point to the word you are saying, and then cover the transliteration. This routine stops the Latin aid from becoming the thing you read instead of Arabic.

Letter details that prevent common misreadings

Hamza is a consonant represented in several written positions, including on or under an alif and on other letter seats. Treat the hamza as a real sound and learn its spelling with vocabulary; do not assume every written alif is pronounced the same way. The ending ة (tāʾ marbūṭa) is often associated with feminine nouns. Its pronunciation can be heard as a t when connected to a following grammatical ending and commonly as a final a-like sound when pausing in careful MSA. Your teacher or fully vowelled text can show the exact context.

Another useful pattern is the definite article الـ (al-). Before a “moon letter,” its l sound is heard; before a “sun letter,” the l assimilates in pronunciation and the next consonant is doubled, while the written article remains. For example, “the moon” is written القمر and commonly read al-qamar; “the sun” is written الشمس and read ash-shams. The spelling and sound are related, but not identical letter by letter.

Common pronunciation mistakes to avoid

One common mistake is pronouncing each written letter as if a pause separated it from the next; Arabic words connect, and some article sounds assimilate. Another is treating symbols such as ʿ and ṣ as decorative punctuation instead of clues to a sound contrast. English spelling can also mislead: kh is only an approximate cue for خ, not an exact English sound. Finally, do not assume that a dialect pronunciation of a letter is an incorrect version of MSA. Label the model you are practising and listen for the intended contrast.

Practise pronunciation without making transliteration the target

Use a three-pass loop. First, listen to the whole phrase without looking at text. Second, read the Arabic while listening again and notice where words join. Third, repeat one short phrase and record it. Compare one feature at a time: a vowel’s length, the difference between hā and ḥā, or the ending of a phrase. A ten-second comparison done carefully is better than repeating a paragraph at speed while the same uncertainty remains.

Transliteration can help you remember a sound, but systems vary between books and websites. One source may write a long vowel with a macron, another with doubled letters; a hamza may be marked with an apostrophe, and ʿayn with a different mark. Choose one system for your notes and be consistent. Do not confuse transliteration with IPA: transliteration maps Arabic writing into a reader-friendly Latin form, whereas IPA is a separate technical notation for speech sounds. Neither replaces listening.

A short practice sequence

Choose four pairs from your current lesson. Write each Arabic letter once in isolation and once connected in a word. Circle its dots, then read the word aloud from right to left. Listen to a speaker and mark the syllable where you hear the target sound. Repeat the word at normal speed and in a short sentence. If you cannot hear the contrast, return to a teacher’s model rather than inventing a mouth position from a written description.

Finish with these two everyday examples: شكرا (shukran, “thank you”) and مرحبا (marḥaban, “hello”). Say each once from memory, then check the script and replay a recording. MSA supplies a useful shared formal model, but pronunciation is not a test of someone’s identity or worth. Arabic communities have many accents; listen with curiosity and make your own intended register clear.
