Hindi Gender — Why Your Verb Changes Depending on Who Is Speaking

A free guide from EkGuru · updated 2026-09-11

A note on the Hindi spellings

These guides are written and typed by hand, and Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong — a missing matra, the wrong nukta, a halant in the wrong place. If a word here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page, and please tell us so it gets fixed. The romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard — Hindi has no single agreed romanisation, so you will see shukriya and shukriyaa both used.

This is the single biggest source of errors for English speakers, and the one thing apps consistently fail to fix. Not because it is complicated, but because it has to become a reflex, and reflexes only come from being corrected while you speak.

The rule that surprises everyone

In the present tense, the Hindi verb agrees with the speaker's own gender, not the listener's.

  • A man says मैं करता हूँ — main karta hoon
  • A woman says मैं करती हूँ — main karti hoon

Both mean "I do". A woman saying karta hoon is not a stylistic choice — it is a grammatical error, and it sounds to an Indian ear roughly like "he are going" does in English.

The same applies to every present-tense verb you will ever use: jaata/jaati (go), khaata/khaati (eat), rehta/rehti (live), bolta/bolti (speak).

Every noun has a gender too

Not just people — objects. A book is feminine, a house is masculine, and there is no logic to appeal to. किताब (kitaab, book) is feminine, so:

  • ✅ अच्छी किताब — achhi kitaab
  • ❌ अच्छा किताब — achha kitaab

The patterns that let you guess

Roughly 80% of nouns follow one of these, which is enough to guess correctly most of the time:

EndingUsuallyExamples
-आ (aa)masculineकमरा room · लड़का boy · पानी* water
-ई (ee)feminineलड़की girl · रोटी bread · चाबी key
-इयाfeminineडिबिया box · चिड़िया bird
-आईfeminineपढ़ाई study · लड़ाई fight · कमाई earnings
-पनmasculineबचपन childhood · अपनापन belonging

*पानी is the classic trap — it ends in -ई but is masculine.

The exceptions worth learning by heart

These come up daily and break the pattern:

Masculine despite ending in -ई: पानी water · घी ghee · मोती pearl · दही yoghurt

Feminine despite ending in -आ: हवा air · दवा medicine · भाषा language · आशा hope

Adjectives change too

Adjectives ending in -आ shift to -ई for feminine nouns:

बड़ा घर bada ghar (big house) → बड़ी किताब badi kitaab (big book)
अच्छा लड़का achha ladka → अच्छी लड़की achhi ladki

Adjectives that do not end in -आ never change: सुंदर (beautiful), खुश (happy), मुश्किल (difficult) stay the same for everything.

How to actually learn this

  1. Learn every noun with its gender attached. Not "kitaab = book" but "kitaab, feminine". Learning the word first and the gender later means learning it twice.
  2. Learn adjective phrases, not bare nouns. "achhi kitaab" sticks better than "kitaab", and it carries the gender with it.
  3. Speak, and be corrected. This is the honest part. You will not fix gender agreement by reading about it — you will fix it by getting it wrong out loud a hundred times while someone patiently corrects you. It is the clearest case in the whole language for spending a few hours with a teacher.

One consolation

Getting gender wrong almost never stops you being understood. Indians hear non-native Hindi constantly and follow it without difficulty. Getting it right is about sounding fluent, not about being comprehensible — so do not let it stop you speaking while you learn.

Quick check

What changes with the speaker's own gender?

The verb — Every Hindi noun is masculine or feminine, and the verb changes with the speaker's own gender.

Are all Hindi nouns gendered?

Yes — every noun is masculine or feminine — Every Hindi noun is masculine or feminine — there is no neuter.

What helps you guess a noun's gender?

The ending patterns — Ending patterns — like -आ (-aa) often masculine and -ई (-ii) often feminine — let you guess most nouns.
