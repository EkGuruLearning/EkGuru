Hindi vs Urdu — What Is Actually Different, and What Is Not

A free guide from EkGuru · updated 2026-09-11

A note on the Hindi spellings

These guides are written and typed by hand, and Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong — a missing matra, the wrong nukta, a halant in the wrong place. If a word here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page, and please tell us so it gets fixed. The romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard — Hindi has no single agreed romanisation, so you will see shukriya and shukriyaa both used.

Two people, one speaking Hindi and one speaking Urdu, can hold an entire conversation and understand every word. Yet they are counted as separate languages, taught separately, and often felt to be separate. Both of those things are true at once, and the explanation is more interesting than most learners expect.

The spoken language is largely the same

Everyday Hindi and everyday Urdu share their grammar completely — the same word order, the same postpositions, the same verb endings, the same gender system. Ordinary vocabulary is shared too.

"My name is Aman and I live in Delhi" is identical in both: mera naam Aman hai aur main Dilli mein rehta hoon.

Linguists often use one name for the shared spoken language: Hindustani.

The script is the real difference

Hindi uses Devanagari, written left to right, descended from ancient Brahmi.

Urdu uses a modified Perso-Arabic script, written right to left, in a flowing style called nastaliq.

This is not a small difference. Two people who speak identically cannot read each other's writing at all. If your goal is reading, you genuinely must choose.

Vocabulary diverges as the register rises

In casual speech they overlap almost entirely. In formal, literary or technical language they pull apart: Hindi reaches for Sanskrit, Urdu for Persian and Arabic.

Here is the detail that undercuts the neat division: most Hindi speakers say shukriya, sawaal and kitaab in ordinary conversation. The "Urdu" words are simply normal Hindi. The split is real mainly in official and literary language, and in the news.

Where the division came from

The same language was written in both scripts for centuries. The formal split hardened in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, when script and vocabulary became markers of religious and then national identity, and it was fixed by Partition in 1947. Hindi became an official language of India, Urdu of Pakistan.

It is a political and historical division at least as much as a linguistic one, and it is worth knowing that before discussing it with anyone.

So which should you learn?

  • Travelling in north India, watching Bollywood, working with Indian colleagues → Hindi. Devanagari is also the easier script to start with, because every sound is written.
  • Family or work in Pakistan, or an interest in Urdu poetry → Urdu. The ghazal tradition is genuinely one of the world's great literatures and does not survive translation well.
  • You only want to speak, not read → learn Hindustani and do not worry about the label. You will be understood in both Delhi and Lahore.

Learn one and you have most of the other for free. Only the script and the formal vocabulary need separate study.

A note for learners

Bollywood dialogue is mostly Hindustani, leaning Urdu in its poetry and song lyrics. That is why film songs are full of ishq, dil and nazar rather than their Sanskrit equivalents — the Persian vocabulary simply sounds more romantic to an Indian ear. If you learn Hindi from films, you are absorbing a good deal of Urdu without noticing.

Quick check

Can Hindi and Urdu speakers understand each other?

Yes — completely in everyday speech — Hindi and Urdu speakers understand each other completely in everyday speech, yet the languages are treated as separate.

What is the biggest visible difference between them?

The script — Hindi is written in Devanagari and Urdu in a Perso-Arabic script; the spoken everyday language is very close.

Where do the two vocabularies differ most?

Formal and literary words — Formal and literary Hindi draws on Sanskrit while formal Urdu draws on Persian and Arabic; everyday words overlap heavily.
