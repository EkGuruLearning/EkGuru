1. The vowels स्वर

11 independent vowels, plus anusvara and visarga chart forms

🔊 Tap any Hindi word to hear it. Too fast or slow? Use the speed button at the bottom-right of the page. This lesson opens one step at a time — press Next to turn the page, or choose Whole lesson to read it straight through.

A Hindi primer always opens with the स्वर (swar) — the vowels. Each one is a sound you can hold with your mouth open: say it, trace it, meet the word every Indian child learns it with.

The letters

This is how the chart hangs on an Indian classroom wall: the letter, and the one word every child learns it by. Say the letter, say the word, tap to hear both.

What to notice

  • There are eleven independent vowel letters plus two chart forms: अं includes anusvara and अः includes visarga. Their pronunciation depends on context. A traditional chart sequence is: अ आ इ ई उ ऊ ऋ ए ऐ ओ औ अं अः .
  • Compare the short/long pairs — अ/आ, इ/ई, उ/ऊ. Vowel quality and duration both matter; timing varies with context, so an exact two-to-one rule is only an approximation. Compare दिन (day) vs दीन (poor).
  • These full letters appear at the start of a word or after another vowel. Inside a word, after a consonant, the vowel shrinks to a sign — the मात्रा — which is lesson 6 onwards.

Hindi vowels: a careful first reading lesson

Extended guided study: English explanation with Hindi models. Native-speaker review is not recorded. Use the existing Next/Back steps or choose Whole lesson to read continuously; no new completion requirement is added.

What this chapter asks you to learn

Your first task is not to memorise a page of unfamiliar shapes in one sitting. It is to connect a small number of written forms with a small number of sounds, and then recognise those connections when the order changes. By the end of this chapter, aim to recognise the independent vowel letters, explain how the three familiar short and long pairs differ, and distinguish a vowel letter from a mark attached to another letter. You do not need to read every keyword fluently yet. Those keywords are anchors for later recognition, not a vocabulary examination.

Use the letter cards above as your reference chart. This extended explanation takes you through the decisions a beginner actually makes: where to look, what to compare, when to listen, and how to check an answer without mistaking familiarity for understanding. You can read the whole lesson or use the existing step-by-step reader. There is no time limit and no automatic pronunciation score. Completing a reading task here does not establish that your speech matches a Hindi speaker. When possible, ask a proficient speaker to listen to one or two contrasts rather than asking whether your entire accent is correct.

Start with the writing system, not the English alphabet

Hindi is commonly written in Devanagari. You read an ordinary line from left to right, and many letters connect visually through a horizontal headline. That line helps organise the writing, but it is not the sound itself. A character may have several strokes below the headline, and a vowel sign may extend above, below or beside a consonant. For now, follow the complete shape of each independent vowel. Do not assume that a small stroke is decoration simply because you do not yet know what it does.

It is tempting to give every Hindi symbol a permanent English-letter replacement. Resist that shortcut. English spelling uses the same vowel letter for several sounds, and English accents differ greatly. The romanisation on this page is a practical reminder, not a separate writing system that you must perfect. Read the Hindi shape first, make a sound prediction, and only then inspect the romanisation. If you always read the Latin letters first, you may learn the English-looking reminder without learning to recognise Devanagari. Covering the reminder for a few seconds is enough to make your next attempt more informative.

Independent vowels and dependent vowel signs

The full vowel forms on this page are called independent vowel letters. They can represent a vowel without needing a consonant as their visual base. You will often meet them at the beginning of a written word, or where a vowel follows another vowel. A consonant followed by a vowel is usually written differently: the consonant provides the base and a dependent sign, called a matra, represents the vowel. These are different written arrangements for related sounds, not two unrelated alphabets to memorise today.

Consider the independent letter आ. Later you will meet the dependent sign ा attached to a consonant. The full letter is not simply pasted after every consonant that needs that vowel. This distinction matters because beginners sometimes write a full vowel letter where a matra belongs, or try to read a matra as if it were a freestanding word. You are only previewing that distinction here. The dedicated matra lessons teach the shapes and reading combinations in order. On this page, identify independent letters confidently; do not add a new dependent sign to your copying practice until you understand which base it belongs to.

Compare अ and आ without forcing an English accent

अ and आ are useful starting points because they make you attend to both a written contrast and a spoken contrast. The reminder a versus aa distinguishes them in this course. The second spelling does not mean that two separate vowel sounds must be spoken. It indicates a different vowel category, commonly described to beginners as the long partner. In everyday speech, duration depends on the word, emphasis and speaking pace. Do not use a stopwatch or assume that every long vowel must last exactly twice as long as every short one.

Say each sound comfortably, then alternate them with a small pause. Notice whether your mouth position changes as well as the time you spend on the sound. English comparisons such as the vowel in but or father are rough starting points; neither comparison works identically for every English accent. If an English keyword causes confusion, return to the Hindi letter, a suitable Hindi audio model and feedback from a proficient speaker. Your goal is a recognisable distinction, not an exaggerated theatrical sound. Next, close the chart and try naming which of the two shapes has the additional long-vowel form before checking the cards again.

  • अ a · independent vowel
  • आ aa · independent long aa vowel

Compare इ and ई as complete shapes

For इ and ई, begin with careful visual comparison. Look at the whole character rather than memorising only its first curve. Copy one large model of each, leave space between them, and point to the corresponding printed card before saying the sound. The course uses i and ee as reminders. These are not instructions to pronounce the names of the English letters I and E. In particular, the short member is not the English word eye. The long member should be a steady vowel rather than a sequence of changing vowel sounds.

A useful recognition exercise is to write the pair on separate pieces of paper and change their positions. Read the left card, then the right, then the right again. If you can only recite the pair in chart order, you have not yet shown that you can recognise either card independently. Do a small number of mixed attempts and stop to compare the shapes whenever you hesitate. Later chapters introduce the related dependent signs, whose placement requires additional attention. There is no need to learn those signs from guesswork now. Keep today's comparison between the two independent forms clear and limited.

  • इ i · independent short i vowel
  • ई ee · independent long ee vowel

Compare उ and ऊ while keeping the sound steady

उ and ऊ form the third pair in this first comparison set. The course reminders are u and oo. Your lips will usually be more rounded than for the preceding front-vowel pair, but you should not force your face into an uncomfortable position. English comparisons such as put and boot can help some learners; they are approximate and can be misleading in accents where those vowels behave differently. Use them as reminders after hearing an appropriate Hindi model, not as proof that the sounds are identical across the two languages.

For the writing task, enlarge both characters and identify what changes in the longer form. Then cover the chart and reproduce just one of them from memory. Uncover the model before repeating the same mistake many times. Correct one feature, copy the corrected form once, and make another delayed attempt. For listening, compare the two vowel categories at the same playback speed. Slowing a voice can make a sound easier to attend to, but it can also change its natural rhythm. Listen at a comfortable learning pace and again at normal pace rather than treating the slowest setting as the pronunciation target.

  • उ u · independent short u vowel
  • ऊ oo · independent long oo vowel

Read ए and ऐ without inventing a universal English equivalent

ए and ऐ are separate vowel letters. The romanisations e and ai are conventional learning cues in this course, but the letters a and i in the second reminder should not automatically send you into the English pronunciation of aisle. In much standard Hindi speech, ऐ is heard as an open vowel rather than the large glide that an English spelling might suggest. Pronunciation varies with regional speech and context, so an accent-specific English comparison is less useful than comparing several clear Hindi examples with guidance.

First practise recognising the two independent shapes. Next, listen to each isolated model if your device offers a suitable Hindi voice. Finally, compare the associated keywords on the original cards. A keyword contains consonants and may contain marks that later lessons explain; you are not expected to decode all of it now. Locate the starting vowel and say which independent letter you found. If you cannot distinguish the sounds reliably yet, record the question you want to ask a teacher: whether your vowel quality differs enough, whether you are adding an unnecessary glide, or whether the audio model itself sounds natural. A precise question is more useful than repeating an uncertain imitation.

  • ए e · independent e vowel
  • ऐ ai · independent ai vowel

Read ओ and औ and separate sound from spelling

ओ and औ introduce a similar challenge for learners relying on English-looking romanisation. The reminders o and au label two Hindi vowel categories; they do not prescribe the full English sound of go or the English word out. In common standard pronunciation, aim to notice the difference in vowel quality without automatically adding a large closing glide. Because pronunciation varies, use these written reminders to organise what you hear rather than to overrule a reliable Hindi speaker's model.

Compare the shapes of ओ and औ alongside the earlier आ. Identify which parts look related and which additional features distinguish them, but remember that recognising a shared visual component does not make the complete letters interchangeable. For a copying exercise, place the three printed forms across a line and reproduce them beneath their correct models. On the next line, change the order. Read your own writing back before checking it against the originals. If your handwritten difference is too small to recognise later, enlarge it and compare again. Legibility and consistent identification matter more at this stage than speed, decoration or imitating a particular printed typeface.

  • ओ o · independent o vowel
  • औ au · independent au vowel

Place ऋ in the chart without overgeneralising

ऋ appears in the traditional vowel sequence and in words that learners encounter, including the keyword ऋषि on the chart. The reminder ri is a practical approximation for common modern Hindi reading, not a statement that this letter has exactly the same phonetic history or pronunciation in every language using Devanagari. You may see more technical descriptions in Sanskrit materials. This beginner Hindi chapter does not ask you to reproduce a specialised Sanskrit pronunciation or apply Sanskrit rules to ordinary Hindi without explanation.

Treat ऋ as its own recognisable written form rather than silently replacing it with another vowel in your notes. Locate it in the sequence, then locate it again when the order is mixed. When listening to the keyword, focus on recognising the opening sound and the printed character; do not assume that knowing the keyword means you understand the remaining consonant shapes. Later in the Foundation sequence, a dedicated lesson returns to the related vowel sign. That is the right place to practise its attachment to consonants. For now, a modest target is enough: identify the independent letter, remember its course reading cue, and know that its dependent form will be introduced separately.

  • ऋ ri · traditional vowel letter; course reading cue

Why the chart also shows अं and अः

Traditional beginner charts often place अं and अः after the vowel letters. It is useful to learn where they appear, but they are not simply two more ordinary independent vowel letters identical in status to the preceding eleven. Each written form combines अ with an additional mark. The dot in the first introduces anusvara; the two-dot sign in the second is visarga. Counting conventions differ between teaching charts, so explain what is being counted instead of using one total as a universal test of whether another chart is wrong.

Anusvara pronunciation depends on its environment and the word. Calling it only the English ng sound is too narrow. Visarga is especially associated with Sanskrit-derived and more formal forms, and its treatment is not uniform across every reading situation. You are meeting the marks here, not learning every pronunciation rule. Compare their visible placement, name the distinction, and return to the dedicated nasal-mark lesson later. Do not erase a dot because it seems small, or add two dots as decoration. Small signs can carry information even when your ear cannot yet analyse their full effect. A careful beginner notices that something is present before claiming to know exactly how it behaves.

  • अं an · a with anusvara; pronunciation depends on context
  • अः ah · a with visarga; formal reading varies

Use the keyword without pretending you can decode it all

A keyword links an unfamiliar letter to a memorable object or idea. For आ, the chart uses आम, mango. For इ, it uses इमली, tamarind. The initial letter is the feature you are identifying today. Some keywords contain consonants or signs beyond the current reading step. This does not mean you have failed if you cannot sound out the complete word independently. It means the keyword is being used as a labelled example rather than as a fully decodable reading test.

Make the distinction explicit in your practice. First point to the vowel letter by itself. Then point to the same letter inside its keyword. Finally say the English meaning of the keyword if you remember it, checking the card if necessary. These are three different tasks: letter recognition, locating a character in print, and recalling vocabulary. If you forget the meaning but identify the vowel correctly, the letter task can still be successful. If you remember mango but point to the wrong shape, revisit the letter rather than assuming that the vocabulary memory proves you can read it. This separation keeps feedback useful and prevents a familiar picture from hiding an uncertain reading skill.

  • आम aam · mango; locate the opening आ
  • इमली imlee · tamarind; locate the opening इ

A short writing routine with an actual correction step

Choose only three letters for the first writing round. Look closely at a printed model, identify its headline and main curves, and copy it at a size large enough to inspect. Then cover the model and write the letter once from memory. Uncover the original and compare. Ask whether a reader could distinguish your form from the nearest similar letter, whether any part has been omitted, and whether the relative placement of the strokes is clear. This is a legibility check, not a handwriting contest.

Do not trace the same incorrect shape for a full page. If the comparison reveals a problem, circle the feature you want to improve and make one corrected attempt beside it. Leave the earlier attempt visible so that you can see what changed. After another letter or a short break, try recalling the first one again. A printed font is a reading model, but handwriting styles vary, so avoid inventing a rigid stroke-order rule from the screen alone. A teacher or a reliable handwriting demonstration can supply movement guidance. If writing is difficult or inaccessible, pointing, selecting letter cards and explaining visible differences are valid ways to practise recognition without making fine-motor control a prerequisite for learning.

Listen deliberately, and know what the audio can tell you

The listening buttons use the site's shared Hindi voice system. A matching Hindi voice must be available on your browser or operating system; a missing voice is not replaced with an English voice and labelled Hindi. Playback begins when you choose it. The system does not listen to you, inspect your mouth or grade your accent. If a voice is unavailable, the printed models, explanations and exercises remain usable. Offline reading can work when the page has been saved, but offline speech still depends on the voice installed on your particular device.

For a useful listening round, choose one contrast, predict the first sound, listen, and compare what you expected with what you heard. Repeat only when you have a question in mind. Endless playback while looking away may make the sound familiar without improving letter recognition. Synthetic voices can also sound awkward on isolated letters or short fragments. If an isolated character is unclear, compare its keyword and ask a proficient speaker rather than treating every machine output as authoritative. You can practise quietly by identifying the printed character; you do not have to speak aloud in a shared room or use audio to unlock any essential explanation in this chapter.

Mix the order to distinguish recognition from recitation

Reciting the vowel row can help organise memory, but it is not the same task as reading an unfamiliar word. In recitation, the previous sound gives you a strong clue about what comes next. In reading, the printed form must supply that clue. After a short chart-order round, mix a small set of letters. Point to each one and name it without starting the entire sequence from the beginning. Keep the set small enough that you can explain your choices instead of guessing rapidly.

Try a two-pass check with the cards above. In the first pass, keep the reading cues visible and compare the shapes slowly. In the second, cover or look away from the cues and predict each sound. When you hesitate, note which comparison caused trouble rather than writing a general judgement such as bad at Hindi. A note saying that you confused the two rounded vowels tells you what to practise next. You can also reverse the direction: look at a reading cue, select a Hindi letter from your handwritten cards, and then check against the chart. Reverse recall is a different demand, so needing help there does not erase the recognition you have already developed.

Fix common beginner errors one decision at a time

If you confuse two shapes, put only those two beside each other and describe one visible difference before adding more letters. If you confuse their sounds, keep the written models visible and ask for a slow contrast followed by ordinary speech. If you recognise the printed form but cannot write it from memory, separate that writing task from your reading practice. These problems need different responses; copying the full chart repeatedly is not a precise solution to all of them.

Another common problem is reading only the romanisation. Try covering it for the first attempt and using it only after making a prediction. If a vowel keyword seems too difficult, identify the opening letter without decoding the rest. If a letter sounds different in a whole word, do not immediately rewrite the chart: context, pace and the quality of the audio may matter. Collect the example and ask a focused question. Finally, avoid correcting yourself solely from an English spelling comparison. English reminders are aids, not a pronunciation authority. Progress here means making a clearer observation, testing it against a model and choosing a useful next attempt, not eliminating every hesitation before you are allowed to continue.

Self-check: explain the distinction before opening the answer

Use the checks below as prompts for reasoning rather than a speed quiz. Say or write a short answer before opening each model. If your wording differs, compare the idea rather than looking for an exact English phrase. A good answer identifies the relevant distinction and does not claim more than this chapter teaches. For example, remembering that a dependent sign belongs with a base is more useful than repeating a technical label without knowing what it refers to.

When an answer surprises you, return to the matching explanation and one printed model. Make a fresh attempt after a short interval instead of repeatedly opening and closing the same answer. These disclosures do not save a score, mark the lesson complete or change your review schedule. The existing completion control remains your own declaration. Use it only according to the learning goal you intended, and keep pronunciation questions separate from a reading checkbox. If you are working with a partner, ask each other to explain why a choice fits; hearing a reason makes it easier to notice when a familiar-sounding answer is based on a misunderstanding.

Does aa mean two separate vowel syllables?

No. Here it is a reading cue for one vowel category, not an instruction to say two separate vowels.

Must a long vowel always last exactly twice as long?

No. Duration varies with context and pace, and vowel quality matters too. Compare clear Hindi models rather than using a stopwatch.

Are अं and अः simply two ordinary independent vowel letters?

No. They combine अ with anusvara or visarga. Their placement in traditional charts should be explained rather than treated as a universal counting rule.

Does remembering the keyword prove that I decoded it?

No. You may remember a meaning or a picture without recognising all the letters. Locate the opening vowel separately.

Can the Listen button certify my pronunciation?

No. It provides playback when a Hindi voice is available; it does not record or assess your speech.

Plan the next two practice sessions

For the next session, choose a small contrast you found uncertain rather than restarting every exercise automatically. Begin with one independent letter, its partner and their printed reading cues. Predict, compare, write or select, and check. Finish by mixing those letters with one that already feels familiar. The familiar item gives you an anchor, while the changed order prevents the exercise from becoming only a memorised chant. Keep your notes short enough to reuse: the two shapes, the distinction you are working on and one question for a speaker if needed.

In a later session, return before looking at your previous answers. Try recognising the same forms in a different order or font size, then inspect the original chart. If the distinction is stable, continue to the consonant chapter while revisiting the vowels briefly. If it is still uncertain, reduce the set and seek a clearer model rather than interpreting the difficulty as failure. New consonants and later matras will give these vowels a broader reading context. There is no need to manufacture a paragraph from letters you have not learned. Careful recognition now supports word reading later, and the existing course sequence provides that next step without requiring you to master every spelling rule on the first day.

Today's task · आज का काम

Read the eleven vowels and two additional chart forms in order. Then write अ to अः once each, saying the sound as your pen moves. Hand and mouth learn together.

Quick check

Which vowel is long: इ or ई?

ई is the long partner. Vowel quality and duration both matter; exact timing varies with context.

What do अं and अः add?

अं has anusvara; अः has visarga. These marks have context-dependent pronunciation, explained in the later nasal-mark lesson.

Where do full vowel letters appear?

At the start of a word, or after another vowel. After a consonant the vowel becomes a मात्रा sign.

A note on the Hindi spellings

Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong when it is typed by hand — a missing matra, the wrong nukta. If something here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page and please tell us so it gets fixed. Romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard.

Read it to a person

Reading aloud to someone who can correct you is worth five silent repetitions. A single lesson with a tutor at this stage fixes the two or three sounds you cannot hear yourself getting wrong.
