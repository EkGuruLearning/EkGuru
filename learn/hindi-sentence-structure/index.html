Hindi Word Order — Why the Verb Goes Last

A note on the Hindi spellings

These guides are written and typed by hand, and Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong — a missing matra, the wrong nukta, a halant in the wrong place. If a word here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page, and please tell us so it gets fixed. The romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard — Hindi has no single agreed romanisation, so you will see shukriya and shukriyaa both used.

English is Subject–Verb–Object: I eat bread.
A common neutral Hindi pattern is Subject–Object–Verb: I bread eat.

This common pattern can feel unfamiliar if you start from English. Work with whole phrases rather than translating each word into the same position.

The basic pattern

For a neutral beginner statement, start with subject → object → verb phrase. This is a useful starting pattern, not a rule for every Hindi utterance.

One idea, three pieces

I · bread · eat

English: I eat bread. Hindi: मैं रोटी खाता हूँ। This example uses a male speaker's form.

01 · SubjectमैंmainI · who?
02 · Objectरोटीrotibread · what?
03 · Verb phraseखाता हूँkhaata hooneat · action

Read the three labelled pieces in order. The verb phrase includes हूँ; it is not a separate object.

EnglishHindiLiterally
I eat breadमैं रोटी खाता हूँI bread eat am
She reads a bookवह किताब पढ़ती हैshe book reads is
We live in Delhiहम दिल्ली में रहते हैंwe Delhi in live are

Notice the last one: दिल्ली में — "Delhi in", not "in Delhi". For these relationships, Hindi uses postpositions, which come after the noun.

The common postpositions

में mein in · पर par on · से se from/with · को ko to · का/की/के ka/ki/ke of · तक tak until · के लिए ke liye for

Every one of them goes after the word it applies to. मेज़ पर (table on), दिल्ली से (Delhi from), मेरे लिए (me for).

Build a full sentence

Context: Use this to describe someone's usual evening routine, rather than to order tea in a café.

Hindi: रीना रोज़ शाम को घर पर चाय बनाती है।
Romanisation: Reena roz shaam ko ghar par chaay banaati hai.
Meaning: Reena makes tea at home every evening.

Read the pieces in order: रीना (who) → रोज़ शाम को (when) → घर पर (where) → चाय (what) → बनाती है (action). The location phrase ends with पर, and the verb phrase comes at the end.

Pronunciation note: In this learner-friendly romanisation, aa represents a long vowel in shaam and banaati; hold the vowel rather than splitting it into two beats. Roman spellings are a guide, not one agreed standard.

Common mistake: Don't copy English verb–object order by moving बनाती है before चाय. In this example, keep the thing being made before the verb: चाय बनाती है.

The trick that makes it click

Stop translating English word by word. Instead, ask yourself three questions in this order and say the answers:

  1. Who? → मैं
  2. What / where? → रोटी, दिल्ली में
  3. Doing what? → खाता हूँ

Use those questions as a practice scaffold. Try saying the parts together, then check the complete example. Fluency develops at different rates; this is a method to try, not a promised result.

Questions

For a yes/no question, add क्या at the start and change nothing else:

आप हिंदी बोलते हैं। — You speak Hindi.
क्या आप हिंदी बोलते हैं? — Do you speak Hindi?

In these beginner examples, the question word appears near the verb phrase. Its position depends on the construction; do not turn this observation into an always-rule:

  • आप कहाँ रहते हैं? — Where do you live?
  • यह क्या है? — What is this?
  • आप कब आएँगे? — When will you come?
  • यह कितने का है? — How much is this?

Negatives

In this beginner pattern, नहीं comes before the main verb:

मैं हिंदी बोलता हूँ → मैं हिंदी नहीं बोलता
In this habitual negative example, the auxiliary hoon may be omitted. It can also be retained, for example for emphasis. Do not apply this omission to every negative construction.

Word order is flexible, but not free

Hindi allows you to move things for emphasis, which English does with tone:

मैं कल दिल्ली जाऊँगा — I will go to Delhi tomorrow
कल मैं दिल्ली जाऊँगा — Tomorrow I will go to Delhi

Neutral statements commonly put the verb phrase last. Hindi also allows other orders for emphasis and in conversation; moving a word can change what sounds natural or what is being highlighted. Practise the neutral model first rather than shuffling freely.

What to practise first

Build ten sentences a day using this frame: subject + place/object + verb. Say them aloud if you can, and compare them with the model. Revisit difficult examples at your own pace; there is no fixed two-week deadline.

The verb endings in these examples change with gender — that is covered in our guide to Hindi gender.

These quick checks refer to the neutral beginner patterns above, not every possible Hindi sentence.

Quick check

Where does the verb go in a Hindi sentence?

At the end — At the end — Hindi is subject-object-verb, so the action is the last thing you hear.

What does Hindi use instead of English prepositions like “in” and “on”?

Postpositions — Postpositions — small words that go AFTER the noun, not before it.

Why do Hindi sentences feel “backwards” at first?

Because the verb comes last — Because the verb comes last, so you wait for the whole sentence before you know the action.
