Spanish Grammar Basics — Sentence Structure Explained

Written by: AI-assisted draft for owner review. Maintained by Prakash. Reviewed by: not recorded. Updated: 2026-10-05. Editorial policy and corrections.

Spanish grammar becomes easier when you build one useful sentence at a time. Start with the shape of an ordinary statement, add a noun with its article, make the verb agree with the subject and then change one part to ask a question. The rules below are a working foundation, not a claim that every sentence has only one possible order.

1. The usual sentence shape

A plain Spanish statement often follows subject–verb–object order, like English: Ana compra pan (“Ana buys bread”). But Spanish frequently leaves out a subject pronoun when the verb ending identifies the person: Compro pan (“I buy bread”). Add yo when you want emphasis or contrast: Yo compro pan; mi hermano compra fruta (“I buy bread; my brother buys fruit”).

Word order can change to put focus on a different part of the message. A beginner should first learn a clear, ordinary order; later, notice how speakers move information they want to emphasize. Do not assume every difference from English is a mistake. The question “what is new in this sentence?” often explains why a speaker chose an unusual order.

2. Nouns, gender and articles

Spanish nouns are grammatically masculine or feminine, and the article usually signals which one: el libro (the book), la mesa (the table), los libros (the books), las mesas (the tables). Many nouns ending in -o are masculine and many ending in -a are feminine, but these are clues, not laws. Learn a new noun with its article and a phrase.

Common exceptions make the point: el día is masculine, while la mano is feminine. Some feminine nouns beginning with a stressed a sound use el in the singular for ease of pronunciation—el agua fría—but they remain feminine, as the adjective shows, and use las in the plural: las aguas frías.

3. Adjectives agree with the noun

Adjectives usually agree in gender and number: un libro nuevo (a new book), una casa nueva (a new house), libros nuevos, casas nuevas. Many adjectives ending in -e or a consonant have the same form for masculine and feminine: un café grande, una mesa grande. The plural still changes when needed.

Adjectives often follow the noun, especially when they identify or describe it: una película interesante. Some short adjectives come before the noun or change form in that position. Learn common phrases as a whole before trying to place every adjective by a formula. Agreement is more important to practise first than the small stylistic choices of placement.

4. Present-tense verbs: notice the endings

Regular present-tense verbs have three common infinitive endings: -ar, -er and -ir. For hablar (to speak), the common forms are hablo, hablas, habla, hablamos and hablan for I, informal you, he/she/you-formal, we and they/you-plural. For comer (to eat), they are como, comes, come, comemos, comen. The pattern carries information that English often gives with a pronoun.

Spanish has regional differences in the plural “you.” Many speakers use ustedes and the third-person plural verb; in much of Spain, informal groups often use vosotros with different endings. Learn the variety relevant to your goals, but be able to recognize the other form. The grammar is not inconsistent simply because a textbook begins with one region.

5. Ser and estar: two useful ways to be

Spanish uses both ser and estar. As a first guide, ser commonly describes identity, origin, profession, time and characteristics: Soy de Jaipur (I am from Jaipur), Es médica (she is a doctor). Estar often describes location, condition and feeling: Estamos en casa (we are at home), Estoy cansado (I am tired).

The “permanent versus temporary” shortcut is not reliable enough by itself. A location can change and still uses estar; a trait can be temporary and sometimes uses ser depending on meaning. Learn high-use pairs: está listo can mean “is ready,” while es listo can mean “is clever.” Context and the verb work together.

6. Negation and questions

For a basic negative statement, put no before the conjugated verb: No tengo tiempo (I do not have time), Ella no vive aquí (she does not live here). Some negative words appear before the verb on their own: Nunca como carne (I never eat meat). When a negative word follows the verb, Spanish commonly uses no as well: No veo nada (I do not see anything). Do not remove one half just because English would phrase the sentence differently.

Questions use opening and closing marks: ¿Dónde está la estación? (“Where is the station?”). A question word has an accent when it asks directly: qué, quién, dónde, cuándo, cómo. A yes/no question can keep statement word order and rely on intonation and punctuation: ¿Tienes tiempo? (“Do you have time?”).

7. Culture lives in the choice of address

Grammar is social as well as mechanical. Choosing tú, usted, vosotros, ustedes or vos depends on region, relationship and context. A textbook may teach one default, but people do not all use the same one. Listen to how someone addresses you and follow their lead; if the relationship matters, asking what they prefer is respectful.

Greetings and requests also carry tone. Quiero un café is grammatically clear; Quisiera un café, por favor is a softer request. You do not need a complicated subjunctive lesson before ordering, but learning a polite phrase as a chunk gives you a natural option while the grammar catches up.

Common grammar mistakes

Beginners often keep the subject pronoun in every sentence because English does, forget the article when learning nouns, and assume every noun ending in a means feminine. They may also use ser for every “to be,” or translate the English double negative incorrectly. Fix these one at a time: say the article with the noun, underline the verb ending, and compare one positive sentence with its negative form.

Avoid memorizing por and para as the same word. Their uses overlap in some translations, but the choice depends on purpose, route, cause, recipient and other context. Learn each with example sentences such as Este regalo es para Ana (“This gift is for Ana”) and Camino por el parque (“I walk through the park”), then extend the pattern from real examples.

Practice: build a small sentence ladder

  1. Start with Vivo aquí (“I live here”). Say it without yo.
  2. Add a place: Vivo cerca del mercado.
  3. Make it negative: No vivo cerca del mercado.
  4. Ask someone: ¿Vives cerca del mercado?
  5. Change the subject and verb: Mi amiga trabaja cerca del mercado.

Write three ladders of your own with verbs you already know. Say each version aloud and point to the piece that changed. Then take two examples to the ser and estar lesson and the Spanish course hub. Short, accurate patterns give you more usable grammar than a long list of names for rules.
