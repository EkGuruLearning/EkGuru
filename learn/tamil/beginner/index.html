Tamil Beginner

From zero: the Tamil script, first words and simple sentences.

Who this is for

  • You know no words yet, or only hello and thank-you.
  • You cannot read the script yet — the course assumes nothing.

The order to learn in

  1. Script + sounds — recognise every letter.
  2. Greetings + pronouns — hello, I/you, where/what.
  3. Numbers 1–10 — then to 20 when they stick.
  4. Word order + “I” forms of 6 verbs — your first real sentences.
  5. Quiz yourself on basics + numbers before moving on.

What you will be able to do

  • Read any word slowly, greet people politely, count to 20, and build simple I-sentences.

Practice

  • Topic quizFilter by topic and level; every answer has an explanation.
  • Review deckThe 20 core words — tick them off as they stick.

Your first 30 days, week by week

Most courses fail because they never say what to do on Tuesday. Here is a four-week plan built on the pages of this site, sized for twenty minutes a day. If you miss a day, do not restart the week — just carry on.

  1. Week 1 — sounds. The pronunciation vowel and consonant tables, two letters a day, said aloud. Add five greetings from the conversation page.
  2. Week 2 — numbers and reading. Numbers 1–20 from the numbers guide, then the tens. Read each one aloud without the roman column.
  3. Week 3 — your first sentences. The grammar page, present tense, I/you/we rows. Build five sentences about your own day, then make each one negative.
  4. Week 4 — real situations. Food, shopping and travel phrases. Role-play them out loud as if the person were in front of you.

What to learn first — and what to ignore for now

  • Learn now: greetings, pronouns, numbers to 20, the present tense, and the words you use about yourself.
  • Ignore for now: literary vocabulary, perfect tenses, regional dialect, and every exception you meet in a grammar table before it appears in a real sentence.
  • Never skip: pronunciation. Everything you drill later is built on how you said it first.
How to know you are ready to move on. You can read ten short words aloud without help, greet someone politely, count to twenty, and build five simple sentences about your day. That is the whole beginner bar — there is nothing mysterious waiting behind it.

How to remember the words

Three techniques do most of the work in the first month, and none of them requires an app.

Say it out loud. Words you only read are stored weakly. Every word on this site has a pronunciation column precisely so you can say it while you read it.

Attach it to something. A word learned inside a sentence about your own life is remembered far better than a word learned from a list. When you meet a new word, immediately say one true sentence with it.

Meet it again. Five minutes of review on three separate days beats one hour of cramming, every time. That is what the review deck and the quiz are for.

  • Ten spare minutes? Read the letter tables aloud and run ten words through the review deck.
  • Thirty spare minutes? Twenty minutes on the current week's page, then ten minutes of quiz questions.
  • A whole evening? Do not study for three hours. Do the usual twenty minutes and watch something in the language instead — listening is not wasted time.

The learners who reach the end of this course are not the ones with the most free time. They are the ones who kept the daily session small enough to survive a bad week.

What this level is, and what it is not

The beginner stage is not a smaller version of fluency. It is a separate skill with its own finish line: reading without panic, greeting people correctly, handling numbers, and building simple true sentences about your own life.

It is normal at this stage to understand far more than you can say, and it is normal to forget a word you learned yesterday. Neither is a sign that you are doing it wrong. What matters is that the twenty minutes happen, that you say things out loud, and that you keep returning to words before they disappear entirely.

When the four weeks above are done, do not jump to advanced material. Take the topics one at a time — food, shopping, travel, time — and make sure each one is usable before you move on. A learner who can really use five topics will out-converse a learner who has skimmed fifteen.

One last honest note: US Foreign Service Institute Category III (about 1,100 hours to professional proficiency) — grouped with Hindi. The small alphabet helps; diglossia (written vs spoken differ a lot) and agglutination (long glued-together words) are the real challenges.
