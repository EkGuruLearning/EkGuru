The Hindi Alphabet: A Complete Beginner's Guide to Devanagari

A free guide from EkGuru · updated 2026-09-11

A note on the Hindi spellings

These guides are written and typed by hand, and Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong — a missing matra, the wrong nukta, a halant in the wrong place. If a word here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page, and please tell us so it gets fixed. The romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard — Hindi has no single agreed romanisation, so you will see shukriya and shukriyaa both used.

Hindi is written in Devanagari, and this is the part that frightens most beginners. It should not. Devanagari is far more logical than the English alphabet: every letter makes exactly one sound, and that sound never changes. Once you know a letter, you can read it anywhere, in any word, forever.

English cannot claim that. Consider though, through, tough, thought — four spellings, four unrelated sounds. Hindi has no such traps.

How the alphabet is organised

There are two groups: vowels (svar) and consonants (vyanjan). The consonants are not in a random order like A–B–C. They are arranged by where in your mouth the sound is made, starting at the throat and moving forward to the lips. Ancient linguists worked this out long before phonetics existed as a science.

Matras — the single idea that unlocks reading

A vowel has two forms. Standing alone at the start of a word it is written in full: आम (aam, mango). Attached to a consonant it shrinks to a mark called a matra.

So क is "ka". Add the आ matra and you get का — "kaa". Add the ई matra and you get की — "kee". The consonant never changes; only the mark around it does.

Every consonant carries a hidden "a" already. That is why क is "ka" and not just "k". This one fact explains most of Devanagari.

The consonants, in their proper order

Throat (kanthya): क ka · ख kha · ग ga · घ gha · ङ nga
Palate (talavya): च cha · छ chha · ज ja · झ jha · ञ nya
Roof of mouth (murdhanya): ट ṭa · ठ ṭha · ड ḍa · ढ ḍha · ण ṇa
Teeth (dantya): त ta · थ tha · द da · ध dha · न na
Lips (oshthya): प pa · फ pha · ब ba · भ bha · म ma
Others: य ya · र ra · ल la · व va · श sha · ष sha · स sa · ह ha

The one thing English speakers find hard

Look at the third and fourth rows. त and ट are both written "t" in English, but they are completely different sounds in Hindi, and mixing them up changes the word.

  • त — tongue touches your teeth. Softer than an English T. Closest to the t in the Spanish tú.
  • ट — tongue curls back to the roof of your mouth. Much closer to the English T in top.

Same story for द/ड and न/ण. Nobody masters these from a chart — you need to hear them, try them, and be corrected. This is the single best reason to spend your first few lessons with a real teacher rather than an app.

What order should you learn them in?

  1. Week 1: the five vowels अ आ इ ई उ, and the consonants क ख ग.
  2. Week 2: matras for those vowels. Start reading two-letter words.
  3. Week 3: the त and प rows — these appear constantly.
  4. Week 4: the remaining vowels and the ट row.
  5. Week 5–6: everything else, plus conjunct letters like क्ष and ज्ञ.

Six weeks of honest daily practice, about twenty minutes a day, and you will read Hindi signs and menus. Not understand every word — but read them aloud correctly, which is where understanding starts.

A common beginner mistake

Do not try to memorise all 46 letters before you read anything. It is demoralising and it does not work. Learn five letters, then immediately read real words made from them. Recognition comes from use, not from staring at a chart.

Quick check

How many letters are there in the Devanagari alphabet?

44 — 44 — each with one sound. That finite size is why the alphabet comes first: once you know the letters you can read anything.

Why is the alphabet the right place to start, before vocabulary?

Hindi is phonetic and finite — Because Hindi is phonetic and finite. Two or three weeks on the letters and you can sound out any word, which makes vocabulary easier, not harder.
