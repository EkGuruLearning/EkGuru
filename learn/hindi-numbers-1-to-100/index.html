Hindi Numbers 1 to 100 — Why They Are Hard, and How to Learn Them Anyway

A free guide from EkGuru · updated 2026-09-11

A note on the Hindi spellings

These guides are written and typed by hand, and Devanagari is easy to get slightly wrong — a missing matra, the wrong nukta, a halant in the wrong place. If a word here looks off to you, trust a native speaker over this page, and please tell us so it gets fixed. The romanised spellings are a guide to the sound, not a standard — Hindi has no single agreed romanisation, so you will see shukriya and shukriyaa both used.

Be warned: Hindi numbers are one of the hardest parts of the language, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling a course. In Spanish, once you know veinte and uno, you can build veintiuno. Hindi does not work that way. Nearly every number from 1 to 100 has its own form that must be learned individually.

There is a pattern, though, and it makes the task far less frightening than it looks.

The tens — your real anchors

These ten words carry the whole system. Learn them cold:

10 दस das · 20 बीस bees · 30 तीस tees · 40 चालीस chaalees · 50 पचास pachaas · 60 साठ saath · 70 सत्तर sattar · 80 अस्सी assee · 90 नब्बे nabbe · 100 सौ sau

The pattern hiding in the mess

Numbers between the tens follow a loose rule: the unit comes first, then a squashed version of the ten. It is the reverse of English.

  • 21 = इक्कीस ikkees — "ek" + "bees", worn down over centuries
  • 35 = पैंतीस paintees — "paanch" + "tees"
  • 47 = सैंतालीस saintaalees — "saat" + "chaalees"
  • 68 = अड़सठ adsath — "aath" + "saath"

The endings are reliable. Anything in the twenties ends -ees, the thirties -tees, the forties -aalees. So even when you cannot recall a number exactly, you can usually tell which ten it belongs to — and in real conversation that is often enough to follow along.

The 9s are their own rule

Numbers ending in 9 are built downwards from the next ten, using un- (meaning "one less"):

19 उन्नीस unnees (one less than 20) · 29 उनतीस unatees · 39 उनतालीस unataalees · 49 उनचास unachaas · 59 उनसठ unsath · 69 उनहत्तर unahattar · 79 उन्यासी unyaasee · 89 नवासी navaasee · 99 निन्यानवे ninyaanave

The shortcut everyone actually uses

Here is the honest truth: in Indian cities, prices, phone numbers and years are very often said in English. A shopkeeper in Delhi will say "two hundred fifty" as readily as "do sau pachaas". Nobody will think less of you for using English numbers while you learn.

So do not let numbers block you. Learn 1–20 and the tens — that covers most daily situations — and pick up the rest gradually. Trying to memorise all hundred before you start speaking is the fastest way to give up.

Two things worth knowing

Lakh and crore. Indians do not count in millions. One lakh (लाख) is 100,000 and one crore (करोड़) is 10,000,000. Written with different comma placement too: 12,34,567 rather than 1,234,567. You will meet these constantly in prices and news.

Half and a half. आधा aadha is half. ढाई dhaai means 2½ and साढ़े saadhe before a number adds a half — साढ़े तीन is 3½. These come up every day when telling the time.

Quick check

Why are Hindi numbers hard to learn?

They are genuinely irregular — Hindi numbers are genuinely irregular, unlike most languages.

What is 11 in Hindi?

ग्यारह (gyaarah) — ग्यारह (gyaarah) is 11; दस (das) is 10.

What is 100 in Hindi?

सौ (sau) — सौ (sau) is 100; हज़ार (hazaar) is 1,000.
