From zero: the Gurmukhi, first words and simple sentences.

Who this is for

  • You know no words yet, or only hello and thank-you.
  • You cannot read the script yet — the course assumes nothing.

The order to learn in

  1. Script + sounds — recognise every letter.
  2. Greetings + pronouns — hello, I/you, where/what.
  3. Numbers 1–10 — then to 20 when they stick.
  4. Word order + “I” forms of 6 verbs — your first real sentences.
  5. Quiz yourself on basics + numbers before moving on.

What you will be able to do

  • Read any word slowly, greet people politely, count to 20, and build simple I-sentences.

  • Topic quizFilter by topic and level; every answer has an explanation.
  • Review deckThe 20 core words — tick them off as they stick.

Your first 30 days, week by week

Most courses fail because they never say what to do on Tuesday. Here is a four-week plan built on the pages of this site, sized for twenty minutes a day. If you miss a day, do not restart the week — just carry on. For Punjabi, anchor the first session to ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ! (Sat Srī Akāl!): Hello! (Sikh greeting). Then keep this Gurmukhi note beside you: Gurmukhi has 35 core letters — the ਪੈਂਟੀ ਅਖਰ (paintī akhar, 'the 35'). It runs left to right with no headline bar, and its letters look rounder than Devanagari. Six dotted letters (ਸ਼ ਖ਼ ਗ਼ ਜ਼ ਫ਼ ਲ਼) cover Persian sounds, and nasalization is marked with ਬਿੰਦੀ (ਂ) and ਟਿੱਪੀ (ੰ): ਮਾਂ (mān, mother) needs its bindi — drop it and the word breaks. Across the border, Pakistani Punjabi is written in Shahmukhi (Perso-Arabic) — same language, different script. Punjabi is tonal: ਘ ਝ ਢ ਧ ਭ and ਹ colour the pitch of nearby vowels — listen more than you read at first.

  1. Week 1 — sounds. The pronunciation vowel and consonant tables, two letters a day, said aloud. Add five greetings from the conversation page.
  2. Week 2 — numbers and reading. Numbers 1–20 from the numbers guide, then the tens. Read each one aloud without the roman column.
  3. Week 3 — your first sentences. The grammar page, present tense, I/you/we rows. Build five sentences about your own day, then make each one negative.
  4. Week 4 — real situations. Food, shopping and travel phrases. Role-play them out loud as if the person were in front of you.

What to learn first — and what to ignore for now

  • Learn now: greetings, pronouns, numbers to 20, the present tense, and the words you use about yourself.
  • Ignore for now: literary vocabulary, perfect tenses, regional dialect, and every exception you meet in a grammar table before it appears in a real sentence.
  • Never skip: pronunciation. Everything you drill later is built on how you said it first.
How to know you are ready to move on. You can read ten short words aloud without help, greet someone politely, count to twenty, and build five simple sentences about your day. That is the whole beginner bar — there is nothing mysterious waiting behind it.

How to remember the words

Three techniques do most of the work in the first month, and none of them requires an app. For Punjabi, make the first remembered item ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ! (Sat Srī Akāl!) rather than a disconnected sound on a list.

Say it out loud. Words you only read are stored weakly. Compare ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ! with its Sat Srī Akāl! reading, then say it again without looking. The Gurmukhi detail above tells you what to notice in the written form.

Attach it to something. Put ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ! into a real scene: imagine the person, choose the greeting that fits, then add one sentence about your own day using a word from the Punjabi vocabulary table. A word learned in a true sentence is easier to retrieve than one learned alone.

Meet it again. Schedule ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ! for a short review on three separate days, then use the Punjabi review deck and quiz to check other words. Five minutes spread across the week is more useful than one hour of cramming.

  • Ten spare minutes? Read the letter tables aloud and run ten words through the review deck.
  • Thirty spare minutes? Twenty minutes on the current week's page, then ten minutes of quiz questions.
  • A whole evening? Do not study for three hours. Do the usual twenty minutes and watch something in the language instead — listening is not wasted time.

The learners who reach the end are not necessarily the ones with the most free time. They are the ones who keep a Punjabi session small enough to survive a bad week: repeat ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ!, review one card, and return tomorrow.

What this level is, and what it is not

The beginner stage is not a smaller version of fluency. For Punjabi, its first finish line is concrete: read ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ! without leaning on Sat Srī Akāl!, handle the numbers on the page, and build simple sentences about your own life in the Gurmukhi.

It is normal at this stage to understand far more than you can say, and it is normal to forget a word you learned yesterday. Neither means you are doing it wrong. In Punjabi, keep the daily session short, say the greeting and its romanisation aloud, then return to the written form. Here is the language-specific difficulty to plan around: Easy for Hindi speakers — familiar grammar, a new script and gentle tones

When the four weeks above are done, do not jump to advanced material. Take one topic at a time — food, shopping, travel or time — and make it usable before moving on. For Punjabi, your check is to read ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ!, recall its meaning, and then continue with a word from the next topic. A learner who can really use five topics will out-converse a learner who has skimmed fifteen.

Treat the difficulty note as a planning cue, not a deadline. In Punjabi, revisit the written greeting and its romanisation each week; notice when you can read ਸਤਿ ਸ੍ਰੀ ਅਕਾਲ! without leaning on Sat Srī Akāl!, then move on to the next line in the table.
