From sounding out to actually reading

The stages between knowing the letters and reading a sentence without effort.

Knowing the alphabet is not reading. Between them lie three stages that most courses skip past.

The first is sounding out: letter by letter, slowly, aloud, the way a child reads. It feels humiliating for an adult who reads fluently in another language and it is unavoidable. Skipping it produces a learner who recognises words they have seen and cannot decode a new one.

The second is the conjunct wall. Hindi joins consonants into single shapes — क्ष, त्र, ज्ञ, श्र and dozens more — and a page that seemed readable suddenly is not. There are only a few dozen common ones and they are worth learning as units rather than deducing every time.

The third is the schwa problem, which nobody warns you about. Hindi writes the inherent 'a' but often does not pronounce it at the end of a word and sometimes not in the middle: नमक is namak, not namaka. There are rules, they have exceptions, and native speakers apply them without knowing they exist. This is why a beginner reading aloud sounds subtly odd even with every letter correct.

Read things you already know. A film song whose meaning you have, a sign you can guess. Comprehension supports decoding far more than the reverse.
