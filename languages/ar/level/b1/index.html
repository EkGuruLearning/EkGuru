Past stories and dual

كان: was and were

كان = was/were! كان كبيرا (he was big), كانت جميلة (she was beautiful). Stories: كان يدرس = he WAS studying (كان + present = was doing!). Unlock the storytelling past!

كان + word / كان + present

كان مدرسا (he was a teacher), كانت جميلة (she was beautiful). كان يدرس = he was studying. كنتُ (I was), كنتَ (you-m were), كنتِ (you-f were)!

Dual: كتابان

Arabic has a DUAL — a special TWO form! كتابان (two books), يومان (two days), مدرستان (two schools-f). Just add -ان! 1–2 come AFTER nouns: كتاب واحد, يومان اثنان!

WordSay itMeaning
اثنانithnāntwo
كتابانkitābāntwo books
يومانyawmāntwo days
مرتانmarratāntwice
أولawwalfirst
ثانthānsecond
بعدbaʿdaafter
قبلqablabefore
Dual with -ان

كتاب→كتابان, يوم→يومان, مدرسة→مدرستان. Verbs too: يدرسان (they-two study)! Dual is everywhere — two eyes (عينان), two hands (يدان)!

noun + ان = two

Watch out:
  • Saying اثنين كتاب — duals: كتابان!
  • Forgetting feminine ة→ت: مدرستان, not مدرسةان!

The dual in real life: pairs

Arabic has a third number. Two of anything takes one ending: -ان when it is the subject, -ين when anything acts on it. Pronouns follow: هما for 'they two' and both verbs agree.

WordSay itMeaning
كتابانkitābāntwo books
والدانwālidāntwo parents
ساعتانsāʿatāntwo hours (o'clock)
هماhumāthey two
صديقانṣadīqāntwo friends
Dual endings

جاء الطالبان (both students came). رأيتُ الطالبَين (I saw both students). The dual also eats the plural: أخوان, not ثلاثة أخوين when there are exactly two.

-ān (nominative) · -ayn (accusative and genitive)
WordSay itMeaning
هما طالبان مجتهدان.humā ṭālibān mujtahidān.They are two diligent students.
انتظرتُ ساعتين.intaẓartu sāʿatayn.I waited two hours.
الوالدان في البيت.al-wālidān fī al-bayt.The parents are at home.
Watch out:
  • عندي أخوان اثنان. → عندي أخوان. The dual already means two; اثنان is redundant.
  • رأيتُ الطالبان. → رأيتُ الطالبَين. After a verb, the dual takes -ين.

Commands and negation

Imperative: ادرس!

Give orders! ادرس! (study! -m), ادرسي! (study! -f), اجلس! (sit!), تعال! (come!), اذهب! (go!). Soften with من فضلك: اجلس من فضلك = Please sit. Command + please = polite power!

WordSay itMeaning
اجلسijlissit! (m)
قفqifstand! (m)
تعالtaʿālcome! (m)
اذهبidhhabgo! (m)
افتحiftaḥopen! (m)
اغلقighliqclose! (m)
اسمعismaʿlisten! (m)
انظرunẓurlook! (m)
Imperative: ادرس/ادرسي

To men: ادرس، اجلس، تعال. To women add -ي: ادرسي، اجلسي، تعالي. To groups: ادرسوا! Negate with لا: لا تذهب! = Don't go!

The imperative alone can sound like an order. Arabic softens it with لو سمحت or من فضلك, and forbids with لا + the jussive — a form that says 'don't', not 'you must not'.

Softening the imperative

أرسل لي الملف، لو سمحت. To forbid, Arabic says لا ترسل (don't send) — lighter than a rule. لا تنسَ أنّ الموعد غداً: don't forget that the appointment is tomorrow.

Arabic B1 beyond the drills — culture, reading, listening, idioms

Culture note

كان is not simply 'was'. It turns the sentence it opens into a scene: كان الجوّ حاراً sets the weather as a backdrop, and a following clause reports what happened against it. Arabic storytelling leans on this 'scene then event' order far more than English does, which is why a learner who translates clause-by-clause produces correct sentences that still do not sound like a story.

Source (named, not linked): en.wikipedia.org, the article “Arabic grammar”.

The 3 mistakes this level makes

  • لم أذهب إلى المدرسة أمس. → لم أذهبْ إلى المدرسة أمس. لم is followed by the jussive; أذهب stays written without the final damma, and the written form must show it.
  • كان الطلاب مجتهد. → كان الطلاب مجتهدين. The predicate agrees with a plural subject: مجتهدون / مجتهدين.
  • إذا ذهبتُ أمس لرأيتُ. → لو ذهبتُ أمس لرأيتُ. إذا is for real future conditions; an unreal or past condition takes لو.
