Portuguese Present Tense — Regular Verbs

Three verb families, one pattern each: -ar, -er and -ir endings, negation with não, and questions by intonation.

Three families: -ar, -er, -ir

Portuguese verbs fall into three families by infinitive ending. The -ar family is by far the biggest and the pattern for all new verbs: falar (to speak) gives eu falo, tu falas, ele/você fala, nós falamos, eles/vocês falam. The -er family (comer, to eat): como, comes, come, comemos, comem. The -ir family (abrir, to open): abro, abres, abre, abrimos, abrem. Same endings as -er except in nós.

Você simplifies everything

Because você conjugates like ele/ela (he/she), Brazilian conversation effectively uses four distinct forms: eu falo, você/ele fala, nós falamos, vocês/eles falam. And since spoken Brazilian nós is usually replaced by a gente (also conjugated like ele/ela), daily speech often needs just three: falo, fala, falam. The full table still matters for writing and Portugal — but speaking is easier than it looks.

Negation and questions

Negation is one word before the verb: não. Eu não falo português (I don't speak Portuguese), Ela não come carne (she doesn't eat meat). Questions need no helper verb and no inversion — the statement becomes a question by intonation (and a question mark in writing): Você fala inglês? (do you speak English?). Add question words freely: Onde você mora? (where do you live?).

The verbs you need first

Priority regular verbs: falar (speak), morar (live), gostar de (like — always with de: Eu gosto de café), trabalhar (work), estudar (study), comer (eat), beber (drink), abrir (open). Note gostar's trap: English "I like coffee" is Eu gosto DE café — the de is mandatory and its absence marks every beginner.
