The Craft of Translation

Idiom for Idiom, Not Word for Word

A master translator asks what a phrase does, not what it says. ‘Break a leg’ is not टाँग तोड़ो; it is ‘जाओ, कमाल कर दो’. ‘दाल में कुछ काला है’ is not ‘something black in the lentils’ but ‘something fishy’. When no idiom matches, translate the function in plain words and keep the tone; when the image itself matters, keep it and gloss lightly.

The three-way choice for every idiom

(1) समतुल्य मुहावरा — a target idiom with the same function and register: ‘the last straw’ → ‘ऊँट की पीठ पर आख़िरी तिनका’ (same image, happily) ; ‘once in a blue moon’ → ‘कभी-कभार / ईद का चाँद’. (2) सपाट भावानुवाद — plain sense when no idiom fits: ‘he's on the fence’ → ‘वह अभी तय नहीं कर पाया’. (3) बिम्ब रखकर टिप्पणी — keep the image when it carries culture: ‘उसने नमक-हलाली निभाई’ → ‘he kept faith with the salt he had eaten — stayed loyal’. Never choose (3) in dialogue subtitles; never choose (1) if the target idiom shifts register (a rustic proverb inside a courtroom speech).

Register and Rhythm Across Languages

English formality lives in Latin-root vocabulary and passive voice; Hindi formality lives in tatsam vocabulary, आप-forms and compound verbs like किया जाना. A legal clause, a tender love note and a street argument each need their own Hindi lexical layer. Beyond words, sentence length and stress must be re-tuned: Hindi carries emphasis by word order and particles (ही, तो, भी), English by stress and cleft sentences.

Carrying emphasis without italics

English: ‘It was RAM who broke it’ / ‘It was Ram who broke it’. Hindi has no stress marking in print; it uses (a) particles — ‘राम ने ही तोड़ा’ (only/precisely Ram), ‘राम ने तो तोड़ा’ (as for Ram…), ‘राम ने भी तोड़ा’ (Ram too); (b) fronting — moving the emphasised word to the start or just before the verb; (c) cleft-like ‘तोड़ने वाला राम था’. Register: formal Hindi prefers tatsam nouns + light verb (निर्णय लिया गया), informal prefers Persian/tadbhav + simple verb (फ़ैसला कर लिया). Match sentence length to genre: legal Hindi tolerates long sentences; dialogue does not.

Subtitling and Voice-over: Compress, Expand, Sync

A subtitle gets about 35 Devanagari characters per line, two lines, on screen for under six seconds; the translator must cut without losing the joke or the threat. A voice-over has the opposite problem: Hindi often runs longer than English, so the dub script must be timed to lip-flaps and breath. Masters cut function words and hedges first, names and numbers never.

What to cut, what to keep

Cut order for subtitles: (1) fillers and hedges (देखिए, दरअसल, मेरा मतलब); (2) vocatives after the first (भाई, यार); (3) redundant auxiliaries where tense is clear (‘जा रहा हूँ’ → ‘जा रहा’) ; (4) one of two synonyms. Keep: proper names, numbers, negation, the punch-word of a joke, any threat or promise. For dubbing, expand or contract with light verbs (कर देना / कर लेना) and vocatives to hit syllable count; place the stressed Hindi word on the visible lip-closure (प/ब/म) where possible.

Anchoring a Stage: Mañc Sañcālan

Indian stage anchoring has a liturgy: welcome, lamp-lighting, introduction of dignitaries in rank order, a couplet before each item, thanks at the end. The anchor's Hindi is slightly elevated, rhythmic, and never upstages the performer. Masters prepare transitions in advance and improvise only the filler when a performer is late.

The Structured Speech: Hook, Three Points, Return

A persuasive Hindi speech opens with a hook — a story, a question or a line of verse — states its one idea, develops three points with a concrete example each, and returns to the opening image in the last sentence. Rhetoric lives in तीन की लय (triads), अनुप्रास, deliberate repetition (पुनरावृत्ति) and the pause. Masters write for the ear: short clauses, spoken connectors, no sentence they cannot say in one breath.

In a Hindi debate or press Q&A the master does four things in order: acknowledge (‘अच्छा सवाल है’ only if it is), reframe the loaded premise, answer in one sentence, then bridge to the point they want to make. Dohā and Caupāī: Metre and Old Grammar

The two workhorse metres of pre-modern Hindi: दोहा (two lines of 13+11 mātrās each, ending the second half on a short syllable) and चौपाई (four quarters of 16 mātrās). They carry Braj and Awadhi grammar: है → अहै/है, का/की → को/केरि, ने absent, -हि/-हिं object marker, verb forms like करत (does), कीन्ह (did), होइ (be). A master counts mātrās and reads the old forms without a gloss.

Knowing Hindi and explaining Hindi are different skills. A master teacher gives the rule in one sentence, then a minimal pair, then a trap and its fix — and never says ‘it just sounds right’. Three one-sentence rules with minimal pairs

ERGATIVE: ‘In the perfective past, a transitive verb's subject takes ने and the verb agrees with the object.’ Pair: लड़का सोया (intransitive, agrees with boy) vs लड़के ने रोटी खाई (agrees with रोटी, f.). Trap: पानी (m.), दही (m.), किताब (f.). COMPOUND VERBS: ‘The second verb adds attitude, not meaning: लेना = for self, देना = for others, जाना = completeness/change, डालना = abruptness.’ Pair: उसने खाना खाया / खा लिया (finished, for himself). Trap: no compound verb with negation in neutral speech (नहीं खाया, not नहीं खा लिया).

Culture note

Mastery in Hindi has always meant moving between worlds. The court poets of Braj and Awadh wrote in metres whose mātrās a village singer can still count; the dubbing studios of Mumbai turn Hollywood into Hindi against a stopwatch; the school annual day keeps a liturgy of lamp, couplet and applause; and the language classroom, from Jaipur to New Jersey, asks the same three questions about ने, gender and compound verbs. A master translator, anchor, teacher or debater is not someone who knows more words, but someone who knows which world a sentence belongs to and can carry it, intact, into another.

The 4 mistakes this level makes

  • He threw me under the bus. → उसने मुझे बस के नीचे फेंक दिया। → उसने अपनी गर्दन बचाने के लिए मुझे बलि का बकरा बना दिया। Translate the function, not the image, when the target has no matching idiom.
  • The tenant shall vacate… → किरायेदार को ख़ाली करना चाहिए। → किरायेदार परिसर रिक्त करेगा। Legal ‘shall’ is binding; चाहिए is advisory.
  • ‘यह नाकाम योजना नहीं है!’ → ‘पहला चरण पूरा हुआ है — बारह करोड़, चार सौ घर।’ Never echo the hostile word; state the fact.
  • Teacher: ‘“खा लिया” बस ऐसे ही बोलते हैं।’ → ‘लेना पूर्णता और अपने-लिए का भाव जोड़ता है — खाया? / खा लिया?’ Give a rule and a minimal pair, never ‘it just sounds right’.
