How to use the transliteration: These Latin-letter forms are a practical reading aid, not IPA and not a universal standard. The list is a curated beginner set of common Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) words and phrases, not a ranked corpus-frequency list. Local dialects often use different everyday words and pronunciations.

Learn words as small, reusable phrases

A list is useful only when words move into your own sentences. Read the Arabic from right to left, say the transliteration once if you need it, then cover it and recall the meaning. In your notes, mark whether a word is a pronoun, noun, verb, question word, or connector. That tiny label helps you use the word instead of simply recognising it. Arabic nouns also have grammatical gender, and verbs change form, so a dictionary entry is a starting point rather than a complete sentence.

The forms below are mostly citation or basic learning forms. A verb shown as “I want” is already a first-person form; a noun may appear without the case endings used in fully formal writing. Pronunciation is presented in a reader-friendly transliteration. Long vowels are sometimes shown with a line, and ʿ represents the letter ʿayn. Other books may use a different system. Keep one system in your notebook, and listen to actual MSA audio for sounds the Latin alphabet cannot capture.

Common mistakes with a new word list

Do not treat this curated list as a ranked frequency chart or assume every English gloss matches Arabic in every setting. The same Arabic word can have more than one use, and a verb shown as “I want” is a conjugated form rather than an infinitive for every subject. Another frequent mistake is learning an adjective without its gender pattern or copying an MSA phrase into a dialect conversation as if the register were identical. Keep a note of the source and setting, then confirm a phrase when naturalness matters.

Transliteration can also tempt you to skip the Arabic spelling. Avoid that shortcut: write the Arabic first, then use the Latin form as a temporary cue. If two sources spell a long vowel differently, the difference may be their transliteration system, not two different Arabic words. Listen to the word and compare the script before adding either form to a flashcard.

Turn a few entries into natural sentences

Start with location words and objects: الكتاب في البيت (al-kitāb fī al-bayt, “the book is in the house”). Arabic often has no present-tense word for “is” in this kind of sentence. For a simple preference, use a complete first-person verb, such as أحب القهوة (uḥibbu al-qahwa, “I like coffee”). A polite request can combine من فضلك (min faḍlik, “please”) with a verb, but the most natural wording depends on register and dialect.

Question words help you recover when you do not know a noun. Practise أين الفندق؟ (ayna al-funduq?, “Where is the hotel?”), كم السعر؟ (kam as-siʿr?, “How much is the price?”), and هل تفهم؟ (hal tafham?, “Do you understand?” to a man). The last sentence changes for a woman in speech and writing with full vowel marks. In real travel, a gesture, map, or translation card can help; these phrases are tools for respectful communication, not a promise that every person uses formal MSA casually.

Practice: a review method that lasts longer than a list

Do not try to memorise all one hundred items today. Choose eight: two pronouns, two question or connector words, two nouns, and two verbs. Make a paper card with the Arabic on one side and a short English cue on the other. Say each Arabic item aloud before flipping the card, then use four of them in original sentences. The next day, retrieve the same items before adding a few more. If you forget, that is information about what to review, not evidence that you cannot learn Arabic.

Sort new words by usefulness to your goal. A traveller may start with “where,” “water,” “please,” “I need,” and “hospital”; a reader may start with common connectors and pronouns. Write a dialect label next to any word learned from a spoken source. Some vocabulary overlaps across MSA and dialects, while other everyday choices differ. Keep the article’s transliteration as a support, check the Arabic spelling, and listen whenever you can.
