Spanish uses the familiar Latin alphabet, but “familiar letters” do not mean “English sounds.” Learn the spelling patterns once and you can sound out a huge amount of new vocabulary. The cues below are broad approximations; listen to speakers from the variety you are learning.

The 27-letter alphabet

Modern Spanish uses 27 letters, including ñ. The combinations ch and ll still occur in words, but they are digraphs, not extra letters in the alphabet. rr is also a spelling combination, used between vowels for the strong trill. Five vowels carry most of the work

Spanish vowels are not reduced as often as English vowels. Keep a open, e clear, i short, o rounded and u steady. In familia, do not turn the final vowels into a vague “uh.” In café, the written accent marks the stressed final syllable. The approximate guide “kah-FEH” is more useful than copying an English pronunciation of “cafe.”

When two vowels meet, they can stay together in one syllable or divide, depending on the letters and accent mark. tierra has two syllables, while país has a written accent that signals the separation of the vowels. Do not guess from English rules: say the word, mark the syllables, then check a dictionary audio entry if the stress is unfamiliar.

Letters that change with their neighbours

C is a helpful example: casa begins with a k-like sound, while cena begins with an s-like sound in most Latin American varieties and in regions of Spain with seseo. In much of central and northern Spain, that second sound is θ. Neither one is the universal “correct” sound; your chosen model determines which you practise.

G has a hard sound in gato but a stronger, breathy sound in gente. The letter j uses a similar sound, as in jugo. A silent u in guitarra keeps g hard before i; the two dots in pingüino tell you to pronounce the u. Learn these as spelling patterns, not as exceptions to memorize one by one.

R, rr, ll and regional sound

A single r between vowels is a tap, as in caro (expensive); rr is the stronger trill, as in carro (car). At the beginning of a word, the single letter is normally strong, as in rojo. The difference can distinguish words, but a learner can still communicate while building the trill. Practise the tongue movement briefly rather than forcing it until your mouth is sore.

In many accents, ll and y share a sound; in other regions they differ. The shared pronunciation is common enough that llave and ya may start similarly, but the exact sound varies. Ask a speaker from the region you are learning, and do not “correct” another speaker for using a local pattern.

Common reading mistakes

English speakers often pronounce the h in hola, turn every written vowel into an English diphthong, or pronounce v as a distinct English v. Another common mistake is treating rr as decoration. In writing, learners also forget the upside-down opening question and exclamation marks: ¿Dónde está? and ¡Qué bien! tell the reader from the start what intonation to expect.

Do not add a tilde to every word that feels emphasized. Spanish accent marks follow spelling and stress rules; they can distinguish words such as tu (your) and tú (you), or el (the) and él (he). The mark has a job.

Practice: decode a line in five passes

  1. Read Mi hermana vive cerca del mercado. once silently.
  2. Circle the vowels and read each clearly: mi · her-ma-na · vi-ve · cer-ca · del · mer-ca-do.
  3. Notice that h is silent and c before e has the regional sound you are practising.
  4. Say it at a comfortable pace, then explain the meaning: “My sister lives near the market.”
  5. Replace one noun: Mi amigo vive cerca del parque. Keep the spelling and stress in view.

Repeat with a short sign or menu, not a page of random syllables. Mark the letters you hesitated over and practise those patterns for a minute the next day. Ten deliberate minutes beats an hour of reciting an alphabet chart without reading a real word.
