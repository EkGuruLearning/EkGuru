If you speak Hindi, Spanish may feel familiar in a few places and entirely new in others. The two languages are related only at a very broad family level: both are Indo-European, but Spanish is a Romance language descended from Latin and Hindi belongs to the Indo-Aryan branch. A few shared international words do not make the grammar interchangeable.

The scripts: Latin letters and Devanagari

Spanish is written in the Latin alphabet, with the letter ñ and accent marks such as the one in café. Hindi is normally written in Devanagari, where consonants carry an inherent vowel and vowel signs can attach to them. Knowing Devanagari will not let you read Spanish automatically, and knowing the Latin letters will not let you read Hindi. Both scripts reward learning their own sound-to-spelling rules rather than guessing from English.

There is one useful bridge: if you already read Roman script for English, Spanish letters will look familiar. But do not pronounce them with English values. Spanish j is not English j, and the h in hola is silent. Treat the spelling as a new system even though the shapes are old friends.

Word order: the biggest early adjustment

A basic Spanish sentence often uses subject–verb–object order: Ana compra pan (“Ana buys bread”). A basic Hindi sentence commonly places the verb at the end: अना रोटी खरीदती है (Ana roṭī kharīdtī hai; “Ana buys bread”). Both languages can move information for emphasis, but the ordinary starting pattern differs. Do not put every Spanish verb at the end just because that is comfortable in Hindi.

Both languages can leave out a subject pronoun when the context or verb form makes it clear. In Spanish, Hablo español (“I speak Spanish”) does not need yo. The verb ending gives a useful clue. In Hindi, the subject is also sometimes recoverable from context, but the verb system and agreement work differently. Learn the sentence frame in the target language, not by replacing each word one at a time.

Gender: shared category, different rules

Spanish nouns are grammatically masculine or feminine: el libro (the book) and la mesa (the table). Hindi also has grammatical gender, so the idea that a noun can affect agreement may not be new. The forms do not map word for word, though. Spanish articles are el, la, los, las; Hindi uses its own agreement patterns with adjectives and verbs. A noun's gender in one language does not predict its gender in the other.

Use that familiarity as a study advantage, not as a shortcut. If you already remember to learn a Hindi noun with its gender, do the same with Spanish: record la mano and el día rather than guessing from the final letter. Spanish adjective agreement is visible in phrases such as una casa pequeña; Hindi agreement has its own endings and positions.

Verbs and the meaning of “to be”

Spanish marks person on the verb: hablo (“I speak”), hablas (“you speak”), habla (“he/she/formal you speaks”). Hindi also marks grammatical information on verbs, but its conjugation and agreement patterns are not the same. Do not transfer a Hindi ending onto a Spanish root.

Spanish has two common verbs translated as “to be”: ser and estar. They divide identity and description from many states and locations, though that shortcut has exceptions. Hindi learners may be used to context and constructions handling distinctions differently; still, Spanish requires you to choose one of these verbs in many sentences. Soy de India means “I am from India”; Estoy en Jaipur means “I am in Jaipur.” The Spanish ser/estar lesson gives a fuller set of examples.

Sounds that transfer—and sounds that do not

Spanish has five steady vowel letters, which many Hindi speakers find easier to hear than English's changing vowel spellings. But Spanish stress, the tapped r and the rolled rr deserve practice. The difference between pero (“but”) and perro (“dog”) matters. Hindi speakers may bring over familiar tongue positions; that can help with some sounds and lead to a Hindi-flavoured Spanish accent in others.

Hindi has retroflex consonants that Spanish spelling does not represent in the same way. Spanish has its own contrasts and regional patterns, such as the s/θ distinction in some parts of Spain and the widespread seseo in Latin America. A pronunciation cue in English is only an approximation. Listen to the variety you are studying instead of assuming one sound belongs to all Spanish speakers.

Social language is not a grammar chart

Both languages make choices about how to address people, but the forms differ. Hindi has आप, तुम and तू with distinctions in familiarity and respect. Spanish has tú, usted, plural forms and, in some regions, vos. Their social meanings do not line up exactly. The use of usted varies by country, community and relationship; do not translate आप to it automatically in every sentence.

Spanish-speaking communities are not one culture, just as Hindi-speaking communities are not one set of customs. Words, greeting habits and expectations around formality differ. If the relationship matters, listen to the local form or ask a person what they prefer. Respect comes from paying attention, not from applying the most formal word you memorized.

What can help you as a Hindi speaker?

  • You already know what grammatical gender and agreement feel like as concepts.
  • You may be comfortable leaving a subject pronoun out when context makes it obvious.
  • You can use your experience learning a script to build a new sound–spelling map deliberately.
  • You know that respectful address is part of communication, but Spanish forms must be learned on their own terms.

Common comparison mistakes

Do not assume Spanish is SOV because Hindi is. Do not copy the gender of a noun from one language into another. Do not use a Hindi word order and simply substitute Spanish nouns. Do not treat cognates or familiar international words as proof that the whole sentence will match. And do not make a Spanish accent imitate Hindi sounds because both languages are spoken in India or in multilingual families; people deserve to be heard in the language they are using.

Practice: compare patterns without translating every word

Read Marta vive cerca del mercado aloud (“Marta lives near the market”). Underline the verb and notice its place after the subject. Then read मार्ता बाज़ार के पास रहती है (Mārtā bāzār ke pās rahtī hai; “Marta lives near the market”) and notice how the verb phrase comes later. Next, write one sentence about your own home in Spanish, with a location phrase, and say it without building it from Hindi word by word.

Make a two-column notebook: “what transfers” and “what I must relearn.” Put vowel stability under a possible advantage; put ser/estar and Spanish word order under new work. Bring the list to the grammar guide and the alphabet guide. Comparison should reduce confusion, not create a third language made of half-Hindi and half-Spanish rules.
